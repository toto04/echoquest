## 1. Anatomy of battle themes: normal, boss, final

### Takeaway
Normal battle themes state their identity immediately, are fast, driving and "peppy" rather than dark, and loop quickly; boss themes keep the drive but shift to darker colour, a heavier texture and more sections; final-boss music is built from fragments of earlier themes and transforms across phases. Harmonically, battle music sits between "dungeon" stasis and functional tonality: one or two chords alternating under a strong rhythmic ostinato. In the published examples tempos run roughly 150 to 190 BPM, which is far faster than a 5- to 8-year-old can echo, so EchoQuest must separate the felt speed (subdivision density) from the echo pulse.

### Cited Findings
- Normal battle themes have "peppy, driving, sometimes even cheerful tones", "tend to loop faster because these battles are shorter", and (commenter Dan Bruno) must "state its theme quickly and directly because the player might only hear 20 seconds of it." Boss themes are "big, dramatic affairs with lots of looming tension or a very different sound texture"; "The keys tend toward less bright/cheery sounds and more towards darker, ominous ones", but "many are often just as driving, beat-wise, as normal battles." — [Musica Mundana, "On Battle Themes and Victory Fanfares" (Chaotic Blue, 2013)](https://www.chaoticblue.com/blog/2013/05/musica-mundana-on-battle-themes-and-victory-fanfares/)
- Adaptive precedent from the same post: the Skies of Arcadia boss track switches to a "crisis" section when party HP is low and an "opportunity" section when the enemy is nearly defeated. — [Musica Mundana](https://www.chaoticblue.com/blog/2013/05/musica-mundana-on-battle-themes-and-victory-fanfares/)
- Form study (Alan Elkins, NACVGM 2020 abstract): dungeon themes are "often characterized by tonally static or ambiguous harmonies and lack of functional harmony"; "Battle themes retain some, but not all, of these characteristics"; town music is the likeliest home of periods and authentic cadences. — [NACVGM 2020 archive](https://www.theunofficialconventionarchive.com/nacvgm-2020-musical-form-and-gameplay-context-in-the-japanese-role-playing-game/)
- Hooktheory transcriptions (crowd-sourced): Final Fantasy IV "Battle" A minor, 168 BPM, verse VI–III6–iv–III6–VI, chorus III–VII ([TheoryTab](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/nobuo-uematsu/final-fantasy-iv-battle-theme)); Final Fantasy VI "Battle" A minor, 168 BPM, i–VII ([TheoryTab](https://hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/nobuo-uematsu/final-fantasy-vi---battle)); Pokémon Red/Blue wild battle 192 BPM, intro/verse I–II in "C Phrygian dominant", chorus VII(add9)–I in Mixolydian ([TheoryTab](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/junichi-masuda/wild-pokemon-battle---pokemon-red-and-blue)).
- Masuda on wild-Pokémon battle music: since the player "may end up catching and befriending the Pokémon they are up against", it is "important not to make the music create an atmosphere that sounds too scary or dangerous"; he calls this "a fine balance to strike." He composes melody first, then bass and drums. — [Siliconera interview](https://www.siliconera.com/interview-game-freaks-junichi-masuda-reveals-composing-process-favorite-pokmon-track/)
- Sakaguchi (Iwata Asks, The Last Story): the relationship between standard RPGs and music "is rather similar to when you listen to music while jogging", i.e. it supports the player's rhythm and energy rather than narrating; Uematsu speaks of "a kind of curse that is attached to music for RPGs." — [Iwata Asks: The Last Story, vol. 2 part 4](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-The-Last-Story/Vol-2-Hironobu-Sakaguchi-Nobuo-Uematsu/4-The-Curse-of-RPG-Music/4-The-Curse-of-RPG-Music-234585.html)
- Uematsu rewrote The Last Story's battle theme to about five minutes and split it into sections used in different battle situations; he built "One Winged Angel" from many 2- to 4-measure fragments recombined. — [Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/final-fantasy-vii-devs-initially-disliked-one-winged-angel-reveals-composer-nobuo-uematsu/) (search extract only)
- Final Fantasy VI's final boss piece is in movements that reuse earlier motifs, "constantly changing forms as Kefka does in this fight", including a countermelody that transposes Kefka's theme and a drop from full orchestra to a "smaller, dinky reed organ." — [Retro XP, "The music of Final Fantasy VI's battles"](https://retroxp.substack.com/p/the-music-of-final-fantasy-vis-battles)
- Boss-music practitioner advice: plan by the fight's expected length; long fights can start slow and build, short ones start fast; multi-phase fights add dynamics, instruments, key change or tempo; adrenaline comes from "a fast drum kit and percussion, with heavy accents on the main beats", with drums placed "slightly before the main beat" (not overdone); transposing the main theme to minor for the villain is "subtle but effective!" — [Game Developer, "Getting/Making Game Music that Fits: Boss Music"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/getting-making-game-music-that-fits-classic-genre-series-boss-music)
- Kondo's three priorities are rhythm, balance and interactivity; "rhythm" means the timing of character movement, animation and the player's button sequences, and music without that link becomes "background music that might as well be piped in from any other room." — [Game Developer, "GDC: Koji Kondo's Interactive Musical Landscapes"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/console/gdc-koji-kondo-s-interactive-musical-landscapes)

### Inferences
- The defining features to copy are drive and immediacy, not speed. At 110 BPM a straight eighth-note bass ostinato on D plus sixteenth-note shaker/hi-hat gives the subdivision density of a 170 to 190 BPM JRPG battle while the echo pulse stays in the children's range from the prior report (100 to 130 BPM). Rule: write the battle at the echo tempo, then add urgency with subdivision, accents and a pushed snare, never by raising the quarter-note tempo past the stage's limit.
- Normal battle loop template (one exchange = 1 call bar + 1 echo bar, 4/4): 8 bars, harmony changes every 2 bars so the call and its echo sit on the same chord: `| Dm | Dm | Bb | Bb | C5 | C5 | Dm | Dm |` (i–bVI–bVII–i, the minor "Aeolian" loop). Static alternative in the Elkins "battle ≈ dungeon" mould: `| Dm | C5 | Dm | C5 |` repeating (i–bVII, the FF VI shape).
- Intro: 2 bars, one-shot. Bar 1: the enemy's signature motif (section 4) in unison low instruments over a D5 hit; bar 2: drum fill with a spoken or sung count-in ("uno, due, tre, quattro"). The loop then starts on its bar 1 with the first call. Never more than 2 bars of intro: a child waiting for a turn needs the beat, not a prelude.
- Boss: same tempo (or at most +10 BPM within the stage limit), heavier kit, timpani on D and A, low brass, and the Phrygian bII from section 3 as its signature: `| Dm | Eb(maj7#11) | Dm | Eb(maj7#11) | Bb | C5 | Dm | Dm |`. Make a boss "longer" by sections (A loop, B "crisis" loop after failures, C "opportunity" loop when close to winning), not by a longer loop.
- Final boss ("Sbuffo" himself): build it from 2-bar fragments of every earlier enemy's motif and of the orchestra's main theme in minor, over a D pedal: `| Dm | Bb/D | Gm7(9)/D | Csus2/D |`; each phase adds a recovered instrument; the last phase strips to one small instrument (the FF VI "reed organ" move) before the victory.
- Keep it friendly by design, as Masuda does: the enemy is going to be won over and recovered, so menace is comic (section 7), and the bass line, not dissonance, carries the drive.

### Gaps
- No primary analysis with measured tempo distributions across many battle themes was found; the 168 to 192 BPM figures are three crowd-sourced transcriptions. The 8-bit Music Theory videos and Ross Mitchell's chapter on rhythm in Uematsu's battle music were not retrievable.
- No source gives a typical battle loop length in bars; Bruno's "20 seconds" is an informal comment.

## 2. Victory fanfares and "try again" cues

### Takeaway
Nintendo and Square victory cues are short (a 2 to 4 second fanfare, optionally followed by a loopable theme), around 150 BPM, often in a compound or triplet feel, and close with the bVI–bVII–I whole-step ascent ("Mario cadence") or I–bVII–I Mixolydian. Game-over jingles are short and slow but not necessarily tragic. In EchoQuest, the literal bVI–bVII–I in F (Db, Eb, F) and a D major Picardy chord both clash with buttons, but both moves can be rebuilt from the safe list.

### Cited Findings
- bVI–bVII–I is nicknamed the "Mario cadence"; the bVII keeps dominant-like 4→3 motion without a leading tone; the progression "lacks a clear leading tone and dominant/tonic relationship" yet pulls strongly to tonic; it typically comes with parallel fifths and octaves; examples include the Super Mario Bros. level-end fanfare and SMB3's airship victory fanfare. — [Game Music Theory blog, "The Mario Cadence"](https://gamemusictheory.blogspot.com/2011/04/mario-cadence.html)
- Super Mario Bros. Course Clear: C major, 150 BPM, triplet-subdivided; it ends with a whole-step ascent ♭VI–♭VII–I; the power-up sound arpeggiates I–♭VI–♭VII; arrangements of it last 2 to 4 seconds. — [Super Mario Wiki](https://www.mariowiki.com/Course_Clear_(Super_Mario_Bros.))
- Final Fantasy (NES) victory: about 3 seconds of fanfare plus a 13-second loopable theme (2 + 8 bars), 150 BPM, "1-Shot + Loopable"; I–bVI–bVII–I followed by alternating bVII and I; "The fanfare relies heavily on conventions, and those are much more inclined to a compound feel." — [Game Music Analysis Club #3](https://gamemusicanalysisclub.substack.com/p/3-victory-theme-final-fantasy-nes)
- Pokémon's signature I–bVII–I (Mixolydian) is described as "a little perilous but overall proud and optimistic", "a perfect color for conveying the feeling of adventure"; the gym theme cycles only I and bVII. — [Splice, "The most important chord progression in Pokémon"](https://splice.com/blog/pokemon-most-important-chord-progression/)
- Game-over jingles (crowd-sourced): Super Mario Bros. C major, 66 BPM, 6/8, about 12 beats, I6/4–IV–bII ([TheoryTab](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/koji-kondo/game-over---super-mario-bros)); Super Mario World F major, 102 BPM, ii9–V7(b9)–I9, i.e. jazzy and major ([TheoryTab](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/koji-kondo/game-over---super-mario-world)); Undertale "Faint Courage" E minor, 115 BPM, 3/4 ([TheoryTab](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/toby-fox/faint-courage-%28game-over%29)).
- Stingers "can either play on top of the current music or can be used to finish a piece of music" (Sweet, ch. 10 summary). — [O'Reilly preview of Sweet, *Writing Interactive Music for Video Games*](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/writing-interactive-music/9780133563528/ch10.html) (search extract only)

### Inferences
- Victory fanfare (non-interactive, inputs locked), 2 bars plus a held chord, at most 4 s; triplet brass and snare figure; three options built from safe chords:
  1. "Mario cadence into an open fifth", staying in D: `| Bb  C5 | D5 (hold) |`. The ascending whole steps Bb→C→D give the Mario lift; the open fifth avoids F♯, so a held or late button press never clashes.
  2. "Brighten to the relative major": `| Bb  Csus4–C5 | F (hold) |` (IV–V(sus)–I in F, the pentatonic world's own major).
  3. "Mixolydian adventure in F": `| Eb(maj7#11) | F (hold) |` (bVII–I in F, the Pokémon colour), with a parallel-fifths brass line.
  Only after the last button press is impossible may a single off-list chord (D major Picardy, or a true Db–Eb–F ascent) sound as the final hit.
- Victory reprise (the "full harmonised" song the child has just rebuilt): 8 bars in F at the same tempo, replaying the child's echoed phrases as the melody with the recovered instrument as lead: `| F | Bb | Dm7 | Csus4 C5 | F | Eb(maj7#11) | Bb | F |`. The enemy's motif returns as a countermelody in bars 5 to 8 (section 4, "redemption"). Length 15 to 20 s, skippable.
- The minor-to-major story is told by moving the tonal centre from D (battle) to F (victory) with the same four pitches, the same trick Kondo uses for modal variety with a fixed ocarina set (see prior notes, `music_theory_note_sets.md` §1).
- "Try again" cue, 2 bars, never tragic: the enemy laughs with its motif on bassoon or trombone, with a fast descending "wah-wah" wobble (the humour category of section 7), over `| Gm7(9) | Bbmaj7 |`, ending unresolved and leading straight into a one-beat snare pickup and bar 1 of the loop. Descending equals "not yet" (section 7); never end on a dark tonic or sudden loud hit. Keep it under about 4 s so the groove is back before attention drops.
- Small per-phrase stingers: success = an ascending button arpeggio (D4–F4–A4–C5 or F4–A4–C5) on bells; near-miss = two soft repeated button notes. Because every button pitch is consonant with every safe chord, a button-pitch stinger fits whatever bar it lands on; that is the main payoff of the fixed-pitch design.

### Gaps
- No study on how long children tolerate or enjoy victory cues was found; the 15 to 20 s reprise length is a guess to be playtested.
- Sweet's chapter on transitions and stingers and Phillips's book chapters were behind paywalls.

## 3. Harmony for tension, heroism and mischief inside the safe-chord set

### Takeaway
The usual game and film devices map onto the safe list with a few clear losses. Available: the Aeolian loop i–bVI–bVII, i–bVII pedal ostinati, power-chord (open-fifth) heroics, i–iv(7,9) rocking, the Phrygian bII (Eb lydian is on the list), F major and Mixolydian bVII–I for victory, and D pedals under moving chords. Lost: the Dorian i–IV with a major IV (G major's B clashes with C5), the true dominant (A/A7 clashes with C5 and F4), D major Picardy (F♯ vs F4), and Lehman's minor-mediant "villain" progression (Dm–Bbm puts D♭ against D4). Off-list colour can still be used where no button can sound.

### Cited Findings
- Lehman on the sound of evil: diatonic material "is perfectly capable of suggesting any kind of emotion", but chromatic notes give "a lot more capability for suggesting the strange, the other, or the marked"; the Imperial March uses "a characteristic progression between G minor and E flat minor" (minor triads a major third apart), and "the way the notes move in a very smooth and snakelike fashion between those two chords can map onto evil characters"; "The Darth Vader progression is sci-fi evil." — [Tufts Now, "Professor Turns to the Dark Side" (2018)](https://now.tufts.edu/2018/01/12/professor-turns-dark-side)
- Mixolydian I–bVII colour reads as adventurous, "a little perilous but overall proud and optimistic." — [Splice](https://splice.com/blog/pokemon-most-important-chord-progression/)
- bVI–bVII–I works without a leading tone through modal mixture; Björnberg's view is that it often replaces iv–V–i or IV–V–I. — [Game Music Theory blog](https://gamemusictheory.blogspot.com/2011/04/mario-cadence.html)
- Pedal point, Alberti bass, arpeggios and counterpoint are standard 8-bit-era devices for implying harmony with few voices. — [The Pixels, on Kirby's Adventure](https://the-pixels.com/?p=138114) (search extract only)
- bVI and bVII "are aeolian chords" giving a dark, "heavy" sound; bVI–bVII–i is a minor "rock cadence". — [samesound guitar reference](https://guitar.samesound.ru/en/reference/cadences/bvi-bvii-i) (search extract only)
- Phrygian is usually described as dark and tense because of the ♭2; Lydian as "wonder and discovery". — [general mode overviews via search](https://bennysutton.com/glossary/modes) (search extract only; pedagogical, not scholarly)

### Inferences
Concrete progressions (bars in 4/4; one chord per 2 bars in echo sections so call and echo share harmony):

| Mood | Progression | Notes on EchoQuest use |
|---|---|---|
| Heroic drive (normal battle) | `Dm | Dm | Bb | Bb | C5 | C5 | Dm | Dm` | Aeolian i–bVI–bVII–i; bass in eighths; brass in fifths |
| Static ostinato | `Dm | C5 | Dm | C5` | i–bVII; best for the first tutorial battles: two chords only |
| Mischief, sneaky | `Dm7 | Gm7(9) | Dm7 | Gm7(9)` | i–iv rocking; pizzicato bass on 1 and the "and" of 2; bassoon staccato |
| Mischief, cheekier | `Dm7 | G5 | Dm7 | G5` | Dorian-ambiguous lift: the open fifth G5 omits B, so the forbidden B♮ never sounds |
| Boss menace | `Dm | Eb(maj7#11) | Dm | Eb(maj7#11)` | Phrygian bII; the only "chromatic" chord on the list; use it as the boss's signature |
| Final boss pedal | `Dm | Bb/D | Gm7(9)/D | Csus2/D` | D pedal in timpani and low strings; upper chords move |
| Victory (F) | `F | Bb | Dm7 | Csus4 C5 | F | Eb(maj7#11) | Bb | F` | relative major, with bVII–IV–I in bars 6 to 8 |
| Try again | `Gm7(9) | Bbmaj7 | (pickup) → Dm` | unresolved, warm, leads back |

- Rules:
  1. Use power chords (D5, G5, C5) when you want "rock heroism" without committing to a third; the safe list is rich in them, and Kondo-style parallel fifths are idiomatic in fanfares.
  2. Put the chromatic or off-list devices (Dm–Bbm minor-mediant slide, A7 dominant, whole-tone runs, D major Picardy) only where no button can sound: enemy intro bar, enemy laugh, boss-phase transition, the final hit. Tag these bars in the cue sheet as "input locked".
  3. Use Eb(maj7#11) sparingly (one boss family, or only in the final battle) so the bII keeps meaning "this one is serious". Voice it with Eb in the bass and the buttons' notes on top (D = maj7, A = #11); avoid stacking a low D against it.
  4. "Ostinato plus changing harmony": keep a one-bar bass cell on D (for example D-D-A-D in eighths) under bars that change upper chords (Dm, Bb/D, Gm7/D, Csus2/D). The pedal keeps the child's tonal anchor constant while the colour moves.
  5. Since Dorian's major sixth (B♮) is unavailable, Dorian "brightness" comes from G5 or Gsus4, and Lydian "wonder" comes from Eb(maj7#11) (A as #11) or F with an added G.

### Gaps
- Lehman's *Hollywood Harmony* and Tagg's *Everyday Tonality* (the scholarly sources for these associations) were not opened; mode-character claims for Phrygian and Lydian come from pedagogical sites only.
- No empirical study was found on how children perceive bII or open-fifth chords specifically.

## 4. Character timbres and leitmotifs: Peter and the Wolf, Kondo, Toby Fox

### Takeaway
Peter and the Wolf works for children because each character is one instrument family plus one tune with its own tempo and character marking, and because the listeners hear each instrument and tune before the story starts. Game leitmotifs work through repetition plus transformation: tempo, meter, instrumentation and harmonisation change while a short recognisable core stays fixed, and motifs are blended to signal relationships. EchoQuest can give every enemy a 3-note signature in its own instrument and transform it (menace → defeated → redeemed) without leaving the four button pitches, by changing the chord under it, its register, its rhythm and its instrument.

### Cited Findings
- Peter and the Wolf (1936) was commissioned by Natalya Sats to introduce children to the orchestra's instruments; bird = flute, duck = oboe, cat = clarinet ("playing staccato in a low register"), grandfather = bassoon, wolf = three French horns, hunters = woodwind/trumpet theme with timpani and bass-drum gunshots, Peter = strings; each character has a leitmotif. Prokofiev's performance notes say the instruments should be shown to the children and their leitmotifs played on them before the performance. — [Wikipedia, "Peter and the Wolf"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_and_the_Wolf)
- The publisher's introduction: "Each character in this story is represented by a different tune ... each tune is played by a different instrument, so each character has its own sound." Each tune is printed with its own tempo and character marking (Moderato energico, Allegro, Andantino espressivo, Moderato con eleganza, Andante pesante and others). — [Boosey & Hawkes introduction PDF](https://www.boosey.com/downloads/peterwolf/introduction.pdf)
- A symphony education guide sequences each theme as a separate excerpt of roughly 13 to 23 seconds, then asks children aged 6 to 11 (grades 1 to 5) to infer why each instrument suits its character. — [St. Louis Symphony, "Musical Characters" lesson](https://slso.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/peter-wolf-musical-characters.pdf)
- Some 4-year-olds can associate the scary-sounding Peter and the Wolf music with the wolf (Trainor & Trehub 1992, as cited). — [Hunter, Schellenberg & Stalinski 2011, J. Exp. Child Psychol.](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/HunterEtAl2011JECP.pdf)
- Murphy (DiGRA 2019) defines a motif as "the smallest musical unit capable of carrying thematic meaning" and a leitmotif as one that "necessarily represents a specific character, location, sentiment"; he lists nine functions: define, differentiate, seed and substantiate emotions, modulate the context of a scene, interweave meaning (Undertale blends the adoptive mother's motif with the adversary's "producing a new composition with hybrid semantic associations"), foreshadow, prime, and constitute an emotional climax (Final Fantasy VIII's love motif first as "a lively waltz", later a "soaring lyrical ballad"). — [Murphy, "How Musical Leitmotifs Enhance Narration and Evoke Emotion", DiGRA 2019](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/1820/1820)
- Undertale motif transformations documented by fans: re-metering ("syncopated" into 3/4, 4/4, 6/8), slowing (one track is another at 15% speed), re-orchestration (guitar, piano, jazz), reversal, and embellished variants across battle, overworld and emotional scenes. — [Undertale Wiki, "Leitmotif"](https://undertale.wiki/w/Leitmotif) (fan wiki)
- Kondo treats the soundtrack as one coherent piece, including transitions and the mix of song types, and waited until he had 4 or 5 songs to present them as a group. — [Game Developer, Kondo GDC](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/console/gdc-koji-kondo-s-interactive-musical-landscapes)
- Kondo's test of a good tune: music "people can listen to over and over again without getting sick of"; he listens "for hours on end. Sometimes I even dance to the rhythm." — [Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/how-does-koji-kondo-make-his-tunes-catchy/)
- Recurring villain themes can be intensified for the final confrontation. — [Game Developer, Boss Music](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/getting-making-game-music-that-fits-classic-genre-series-boss-music)

### Inferences
- One character = one instrument family + one 3-note cell + one rhythm + one register + one tempo word. Make two of these five differ between any two enemies (the prior report's "never share both pitch and rhythm" rule, extended to timbre).
- Introduce before you fight (Prokofiev's own advice): before each battle, Maestro Battuta presents the enemy's instrument and motif in isolation ("Ascolta: questo è il Tamburone!"), then the battle begins. This doubles as a pre-listening of the timbre the child must track.
- Signature design (original examples, buttons as 1 = D4, 2 = F4, 3 = A4, 4 = C5):
  - Sbuffo (the wind): cell 4-3-1 (C5 A4 D4, a falling sweep) on flute with flutter-tongue, preceded by a non-button slide-whistle "whoosh" up into the first note; rhythm short-short-long. The glide is his alone and appears only in input-locked moments.
  - Maestro Battuta: 1-3 (D4 up to A4, an open fifth) as two firm quarter notes on brass, like a baton tap; doubles as the "pronti?" cue.
  - Enemy-instrument characters (piano, drum, bell): each keeps its own timbre for its call; the recovered instrument then becomes the layer added in the next battles (instrument = character = stem).
- Transformation table for an enemy motif (same pitches, different setting):

| State | Harmony under the cell | Register / articulation | Tempo / meter | Instrument |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Menace (battle) | Dm or Eb(maj7#11) | low octave, staccato | loop tempo, straight 4/4 | enemy instrument + low brass doubling |
| Taunt (child failed) | Gm7(9) | fast wobble ending | same | bassoon / trombone |
| Defeated | Bbmaj7 | high octave, legato | half tempo | the same instrument, alone and soft |
| Redeemed (victory reprise) | F, Bb | middle, countermelody | 6/8 or swung | joins the orchestra |
| Overworld hint | F6 or Dm7 | distant (reverb, low level) | slow | music-box or celesta |

  Changing the chord under fixed notes is the cheapest major/minor flip: D-F-A over Dm sounds like the enemy, over Bbmaj7 (as 3rd, 5th, maj7) it sounds tender.
- Interweave in the final battle and finale: the last victory reprise layers every redeemed enemy motif as a countermelody (the Undertale "hybrid" function), one per 2 bars, so the child hears the whole cast return.
- Keep the orchestra's main theme short (2 bars) and present everywhere (title, hub, victory), so that its minor version in the final battle is recognised (the "transpose the main theme to minor" device).

### Gaps
- No score-level analysis of the Peter and the Wolf themes was opened; the tempo markings could not be reliably paired with characters from the PDF layout.
- No empirical study was found on how many leitmotifs children aged 5 to 12 can learn in a game; the cast size should stay small (one new motif per chapter) until tested.

## 5. Adaptive music: layering, re-sequencing, quantised transitions, stingers, loops

### Takeaway
The standard toolkit is vertical layering (stems that each sound good alone and in any combination, faded in and out), horizontal re-sequencing (switching or stitching segments at musically valid boundaries, often via transition segments), stingers (short phrases over the music, in key, optionally quantised to a beat or phrase end) and seamless loops (tails baked into the loop start, intro and loop as separate files, scheduled sample-accurately). A hybrid of the two is common: Sackboy used horizontal segments for progress, a choir layer for variety and a "success" melody layer that fits every segment harmonically. For EchoQuest's success-adds-layers design, layering is the core and horizontal changes happen only at phrase boundaries.

### Cited Findings
- Vertical layering is "the playing of multiple independent audio files simultaneously within the game's audio engine"; layers are "not always meant to play simultaneously" and "can play in multiple configurations"; it is "fundamentally different than the process of simply preparing stems for mixing"; in Spyder it "indicated progress through the level, and it created variety". — [Phillips, "Pure vertical layering" (GDC 2021 series, Game Developer)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/blogs/pure-vertical-layering-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-)
- LittleBigPlanet's brief requires all interactive tracks to be vertically layered; "Every layer in a Vertical Layering composition can have a very distinct musical identity"; "Played separately, we hear submixes that are still satisfying and entertaining for their own sake"; activations "would occur with smooth crossfade transitions." — [Phillips, LittleBigPlanet 3 GDC talk write-up](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/interactive-game-music-of-littlebigplanet-3-concepts-from-the-gdc-talk-)
- Sackboy hybrid: seven horizontal segments for level progress, a choir layer added or removed for novelty, and a success melody for each Dreamer Orb that "merged harmonically with whatever horizontal segment" was playing. — [Phillips, "Hybrid horizontal/vertical structure"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/hybrid-horizontal-vertical-structure-for-game-music-composers-from-spyder-to-sackboy-gdc-2021-)
- Sweet (Berklee notes): "A stinger is a short musical phrase that's played on top of the music to signal a game event"; stingers must not clash with the current key and progression; they "can also be played on a specific beat or at the end of a musical phrase"; looping means repeating "without the player hearing any loop point"; "Vertical remixing refers to the ability to add or remove instrument layers"; layers are preferred over cue switching for rapid state changes; cue switches can wait for "the next downbeat or musical phrase". — [Berklee Online Take Note, Michael Sweet](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/?p=14985)
- Kondo: Super Mario World's percussion track switches with whether Mario rides Yoshi; Twilight Princess adds a band as the player reaches the town centre; for Ocarina's Hyrule Field, "12 phrases were randomly chosen and stitched together to create an 8 phrase melody". — [Game Developer, Kondo GDC](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/console/gdc-koji-kondo-s-interactive-musical-landscapes)
- Wwise practice: "Next Bar" exits change on bar completion; the entry cue is the sync point, so intro (pre-entry) and loop should be separate segments. — [Audiokinetic Q&A](https://www.audiokinetic.com/qa/1367/issue-with-pre-entry-when-using-states-to-change-the-music) and [Clocker case study](https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/blog/the-sound-design-of-clocker-part2) (search extract only)
- FMOD: transition regions take a quantization interval, and the playhead moves to the destination at the next quantization point. — [Hades II modding wiki](https://sgg-modding.github.io/Hades2ModWiki/docs/creating-mods/audio/adding-dynamic-music) (search extract only; official FMOD manual pages did not render)
- Rhythm Quest: musical callouts map directly to actions, typically two instruments for two actions; "These empty spaces without notes provide players with "lookahead" time to prepare"; "Call-and-response melodies are really good for this as they provide natural breaks"; a song "that constantly throws notes at you with no chance to rest will be perceived as more difficult"; structure intro, full section, lighter verse, build-up, climax, ramp-down, outro supports "flow state". — [Rhythm Quest devlog 13](https://rhythmquestgame.com/devlog/13.html)
- Unity: `AudioSource.PlayScheduled` plays at an absolute time on the `AudioSettings.dspTime` timeline; schedule "slightly in the future (~100-200ms)"; it "is the preferred way to stitch AudioClips in music players because it is independent of the frame rate"; the official example alternates two AudioSources scheduling each 16-bar segment about 1 s ahead. — [Unity 6000.6 Scripting API, PlayScheduled](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.6/script-reference/unityengine/audiosource/playscheduled)
- Loop seams: cut at a zero crossing, move the last bars (tail) to the start and bounce, crossfade the join, add ~10-sample fades at the file edges; if no clean cut exists, compose "post-roll" bars as a transition. — [MakeUseOf, "How to create a music loop for video games"](https://www.makeuseof.com/how-to-create-music-loop-video-games/). MP3 imports rarely loop cleanly because of frame padding; use WAV or a loop-safe format. — [Unity forum thread](https://discussions.unity.com/t/need-help-getting-audio-to-loop-seamlessly-a-certain-amount-of-times/1719532) (search extract only)

### Inferences
- Battle stem set (all 8 bars, same tempo, all ending with tails folded into bar 1):
  - L0 Groove (always on, including during the call): kick, snare, shaker in 16ths, bass ostinato on the chord roots, no pitched material between D3 and C6.
  - L1 Harmony: soft pad or guitar comping, low (D3 to C4) or high (above C6), never sustaining in D4 to C5.
  - L2 Companion: an instrument that repeats the child's echo one exchange later (the Patapon "tribe sings back" role); added on the first success.
  - L3 Hits: brass stabs and timpani on bar downbeats; added on the second consecutive success.
  - L4 Lead or "fever": the orchestra's main theme fragment over the loop, played only in echo-free bars (bars 7 to 8, or as a 2-bar solo after a phrase); added on a streak.
  - Recovered instrument stems: each defeated enemy's instrument becomes a permanent extra layer in later battles (the collection is audible).
  Each layer must sound complete with L0 alone (LittleBigPlanet rule), so any subset is a valid mix.
- Layer changes: fade in over one beat starting at the next bar line after the success is judged; on failure, remove only the top layer, also at the bar line. Never change layers in the middle of a call or echo bar.
- Horizontal changes (A loop → crisis loop → opportunity loop → victory) happen only at the next 2-bar exchange boundary, via a 1-bar transition or a stinger, scheduled with `PlayScheduled` on the dspTime grid. The victory transition waits for the end of the echo bar, then plays the fanfare on the next downbeat.
- Stingers are built only from button pitches and the current chord's power-chord tones (root and fifth), so they can be fired at any beat without harmonic logic; quantise them to the next beat (feedback) or the next downbeat (phase change).
- Avoid repetition fatigue the Kondo way: write 3 or 4 interchangeable 2-bar variants of L1 and L3 per chord pair and stitch them randomly at exchange boundaries; because the chord grid and the button set are fixed, any variant fits.
- Loop hygiene: deliver intro, loop and outro as separate WAV files; render every stem with its tail past the loop end and fold it into bar 1; align all stems sample-exactly at the same length; keep reverb sends short in the call register so tails don't smear the next call.

- Official FMOD and Wwise manual pages did not render in this session; Phillips's *A Composer's Guide to Game Music*, Sweet's book and Collins's *Game Sound* were not available in full text.
- No GDC source was retrieved on Hi-Fi Rush's music system; the GDC 2024 talk ("Developing Hi-Fi RUSH Backwards") is listed in the [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1034256/Developing-Hi-Fi-RUSH-Backwards) but was not watched.

## 6. Arranging and mixing for clarity in audio-only play

### Takeaway
Children need larger acoustic differences than adults to hear two streams, suffer more from informational masking until about 10 to 11, and gain less from spatial separation at age 5; repeating a target helps them more than adults. So the call must be separated mainly by register, timbre and silence (arrangement), with ducking and panning as support. Loudness conventions are about -24 LUFS (console) and -18 LUFS (portable) integrated with a -1 dBTP ceiling; mono and small-speaker safety and per-category volume control are accessibility basics. Blind players in one study did not treat music as an information channel unless it was composed to carry information.

### Cited Findings
- Development of stream segregation (mini-review): mechanisms are present from birth but keep maturing; children need larger acoustic differences than adults, which "remains larger in late childhood than in adulthood"; informational masking: "4- to 10-year-old children showed more masking than adults" while 11- to 16-year-olds did not differ; "4- to 7-year-olds had poorer thresholds than 8- to 11-year-olds and adults", attributed to "an inability to selectively attend to the target while ignoring the distractor"; "5-year-olds show less benefit from spatial cues"; infants prefer the highest voice in a mixture. — [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2024 mini-review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2024.1352247/full)
- Repetition helps children more: in a random-frequency masker, improvement in detecting a 1000-Hz tone from two to four signal bursts was 15.6 dB (ages 5 to 7), 14.2 dB (8 to 10) and 9.5 dB (adults); thresholds of the younger children were still significantly higher than adults'. — [Leibold & Bonino 2009, JASA (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2736737)
- Westerholm, Tuuri and Hassan (Mindtrek 2024; 6 blind or visually impaired and 6 sighted participants): "music generally did not play a significant accessibility role for VIB" players, but a composition designed to convey environmental information and player-status feedback was highly appreciated. — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_98477)
- Kondo's "balance" includes balancing frequencies and music against sound effects. — [Game Developer, Kondo GDC](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/console/gdc-koji-kondo-s-interactive-musical-landscapes)
- AFB: when a game speaks useful information, background music may interfere; let players adjust or switch off music and effects, ideally with an in-game toggle. — [AFB AccessWorld, gaming accessibility](https://afb.org/aw/winter2024/accessibility-gaming-deaf-blind) (search extract only)
- Mono output helps players with unilateral hearing loss and "should ideally be set at platform level"; speaker balance helps partial unilateral loss. — [IGDA GA-SIG, stereo/mono toggle](https://igda-gasig.org/about-game-accessibility/platform-level-accessibility-recommendations/stereomono-toggle-leftright-speaker-balance)
- Loudness: the G.A.N.G. IESD recommends -23 LUFS ±2 (aligned with Sony's paper), measured with ITU-R BS.1770 over at least 30 minutes of representative play, true peak "not north of -1dBFS", 79 dB monitoring reference; Sony's portable (Vita) figure is -18 LUFS. — [Designing Sound, "Loudness in game audio" (2013)](https://designingsound.org/2013/02/loudness-in-game-audio/). Later Sony ASWG-R001 figures are -24 ±2 LKFS for home and -18 ±2 LKFS for portable, -1 dBTP (search extract, matching the [ITU-R BS.2434 report](https://www.itu.int/dms_pub/itu-r/opb/rep/R-REP-BS.2434-2018-PDF-E.pdf), not opened).
- Ducking conventions from game-mix guides: music and ambience down about 6 to 9 dB under voice, with a fast attack and gentle release; priority order voice, then gameplay-critical cues, then music and ambience. — [Bugnet, "How to mix game audio so nothing drowns out"](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-to-mix-game-audio-so-nothing-drowns-out) (search extract only; blog-level source)
- Arrangement before EQ: fix masking by arranging and balancing first; several instruments doubling a lead in the same octave give "little separation". — [Mastering.com, separation and clarity](https://mastering.com/separation-clarity/) (search extract only)
- Phones are close to mono when held upright; sum low end (about 100 Hz and below) to mono and avoid phase-based widening. — [Sonible, stereo to mono](https://www.sonible.com/blog/stereo-to-mono/) (search extract only)
- Children's headphone exposure: WHO sets 75 dB(A) for "sensitive users" such as children; many "85 dB-limited" children's headphones do not limit. — already sourced in `research_notes/Audio rhythm game music design/audio_games_accessibility.md` §3.

### Inferences
- Frequency map (fundamentals): the buttons are D4 294 Hz, F4 349 Hz, A4 440 Hz, C5 523 Hz. Reserve roughly 260 to 600 Hz (and their first harmonics up to about 1.2 kHz) for the call, the echo and companion. Bass sits at D1 to D3 (37 to 147 Hz) with added upper harmonics (saturation, a clicky attack) so it survives tablet speakers; pads sit below C4 or above C6; percussion stays unpitched (shaker, hats, wood).
- Highest-voice rule: during listen turns nothing pitched sits above the call (infants and children track the top voice). Sparkle comes from noise-based percussion, not bells above C5.
- Listen turn = sparse turn: drop L1 to L4 to about -9 dB, or mute them, for the call bar; keep L0 only; restore at the echo bar. With layers designed to stand alone (section 5), this is an arrangement change, not a mix hack.
- Timbre contrast: enemy calls on bright, fast-attack instruments (plucked, struck, short brass); backing on soft-attack or noise-based sounds. The child's button timbre is a third, fixed colour.
- Repetition as clarity: for ages 5 to 7, play each new call twice before the first echo (the Leibold and Bonino burst effect), then once when it is familiar.
- Narration: Maestro Battuta never speaks over a call; voice ducks music by about 6 to 9 dB with fast attack and gentle release; voice lines go in input-free bars.
- Panning: keep call, echo and voice centred (the 5-year-old spatial-cue finding and mono safety); use stereo only for width in L1 and L3; check every battle in mono summed to one small speaker.
- Loudness: master each battle cue and the full game mix to about -18 LUFS integrated (portable convention; most children will play on tablets, laptops or limited headphones), -1 dBTP; keep cue-to-cue variation within about ±2 LU so victory does not jump in level; expose separate music, effects and voice sliders plus a "music off, beat only" option for the echo.
- Music as information (Westerholm et al.): the layer count itself tells the child how the battle is going (fuller = winning), the boss "crisis" or "opportunity" loop tells the state, and the bII chord means "boss". Name these meanings once in the tutorial story.

### Gaps
- No numeric threshold (semitones or dB) was retrieved for children's melody-in-music segregation; the 260 to 600 Hz reservation is arithmetic, not measured.
- No published loudness spec for children's games or for audio-only games was found.
- No guidance from A Blind Legend, The Vale or Papa Sangre developers on music versus information sounds was retrievable in this session; an interview with the A Blind Legend team exists in French ([NRJ](https://www.nrj.fr/actus/a-blind-legend-un-jeu-video-a-jouer-les-yeux-fermes-mais-vraiment-311999)) but only the questions were visible.

## 7. Emotion and age-appropriate intensity

### Takeaway
Children read musical emotion first through tempo and arousal and only later through mode: 5-year-olds respond to tempo only, 6 to 8 to both tempo and mode, and identification is adult-like by about 11. All children prefer high-arousal music (happy and scary), and "scary" is the high-arousal negative category, so the target is high arousal with positive valence. Cartoon-humour signs (fast, low brass glissandi and wobbles) signal light-hearted conflict; horror signs (slow ascending high-string glissandi, sustained dissonance) should be avoided.

### Cited Findings
- Dalla Bella, Peretz, Rousseau and Gosselin (Cognition, 2001): 32 excerpts manipulated in tempo and mode; 6- to 8-year-olds used both cues like adults, 5-year-olds used tempo only, 3- to 4-year-olds were at chance; "tempo is mastered earlier than mode to infer the emotional tone conveyed by music." — [Europe PMC record of PMID 11274986](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:11274986%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json)
- Hunter, Schellenberg and Stalinski (2011), ages 5, 8, 11 and adults, happy/scary/peaceful/sad excerpts: high-arousal emotions (happiness, fear) were identified better than low-arousal ones, more so in younger children; "children of all ages preferred excerpts depicting high-arousal emotions" while adults favoured positive ones; accuracy for scary excerpts was 63% at 5 (with 18.7% labelled "happy"), 80% at 8, 87% at 11; adult-like by 11. The authors attribute younger children's reliance on tempo to arousal cues being near-universal while mode is culture-specific and learned. — [Hunter et al. 2011 (author PDF)](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/HunterEtAl2011JECP.pdf)
- Glissandi in games fall into four semiotic groups: movement or status, humour, horror and suspense, and the paranormal; humour glissandi are "often associated with brass instruments in a low register, particularly trombone", fast, frequent, often up-and-down "as a wobble effect"; horror glissandi are "usually only ascending and slowly drawn out in the high-register strings", saved for special moments; ascending glissandi signal something getting better (for example Pokémon healing), descending ones something getting worse; classic cartoons used slide whistles or trombones "to signify action or violence in a light-hearted, humorous way". — [Smith, "The Semiotics of Glissandi in Video Games", Music Theory Online 32.1 (2026)](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.26.32.1/mto.26.32.1.smith.pdf)
- Mickey-mousing is "the split-second synchronizing of musical and visual action" (Brown 1994, as quoted). — [Smith 2026](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.26.32.1/mto.26.32.1.smith.pdf)
- Masuda keeps wild-battle music from sounding "too scary or dangerous" because the opponent may become a friend. — [Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/interview-game-freaks-junichi-masuda-reveals-composing-process-favorite-pokmon-track/)

### Inferences
- For ages 5 to 6, minor mode alone will not make a battle feel threatening (they read tempo, not mode), so D minor is safe for the youngest. Menace for them must come from timbre and register (low bassoon, tuba, timpani), and fun from tempo and bounce. Keep battle tempos at or above about 100 BPM so the overall affect reads as excitement, not sadness.
- Avoid the full "scary" bundle at once: slow + minor + low + sustained dissonance + sudden loud hits. Any one or two is fine (low + minor + staccato at 110 BPM is playful-menacing); all of them together is horror.
- "Playful-menacing" palette: staccato low woodwinds and brass, pizzicato bass, timpani, woodblock, trombone and slide-whistle wobbles, a bII chord used sparingly, comic rests (a sudden one-beat stop before the enemy's call, the Tom-and-Jerry tiptoe). Forbidden palette: slowly rising high-string glissandi, clusters, drones on a semitone, screams or breathy whispers, jump-scare stingers, sub-bass rumbles without a beat.
- Use glissando direction consistently as status: ascending glides and arpeggios = success, layer gained, instrument recovered; descending wobble = miss, try again. Never use a slow descending minor line for a failure (that reads as sad, not funny).
- Scale menace by age band (the prior report's stages): stages 0 to 1 get "mischief" progressions only (Dm7–Gm7(9), Dm–C5) and comic enemies; the Eb(maj7#11) boss colour and denser percussion come from stage 2 (6 to 8, when mode starts to matter); the final-battle pedal build and minor main theme from stage 3 up.
- Always resolve the arc into major-mode joy (F-centred victory reprise) so every scary-ish encounter ends happy within the same session.

### Gaps
- The emotion studies used Western classical excerpts and sighted children; no data on blind children's music-emotion recognition was retrieved in this session.
- No study was found on children's enjoyment of "scary but funny" music specifically (the sweet spot is inferred from the arousal preference and cartoon practice).
