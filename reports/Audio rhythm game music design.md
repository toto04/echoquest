# Four consonant notes can carry EchoQuest

EchoQuest can get Ocarina-style musical depth from four buttons by fixing one semitone-free, tritone-free note set (la, do, mi, so, voiced so that every neighbouring pair is at least a minor third apart), keeping it on the same buttons and the same absolute pitches for the whole game, and letting contour, rhythm and a changing bass, not extra pitches, make each song distinct. That is how Ocarina of Time gets twelve melodies out of five notes, and it matches what the psychology says: children and untrained listeners encode the up-and-down shape and rhythm of a short tune before its exact intervals. The battle loop that best fits the evidence is a strict call-and-response over a backing beat that never stops, judged per phrase rather than per note, with every press sounding a pleasant note even when it is wrong. Timing should be judged by relative rhythm anchored on the child's own first press for the youngest players, which also cancels Bluetooth latency, and by absolute windows of roughly ±150 ms (ages 5 to 7) and ±100 ms (ages 8 to 12) only later. Buttons should carry all dense rhythm; tilt, shake and hold should be reserved for slow, expressive actions, and the two vibration motors should act as a redundant beat and confirmation channel, never as the only cue. The evidence base has a large hole that shapes everything: almost all developmental data come from sighted children, almost all auditory-display and haptic data from adults, and no study of rhythm synchronisation in blind children was found, so every number here is a starting value for playtesting, not a specification.

**How to read this report.** Each section separates what the cited sources support from what is a design proposal. The researchers' own confidence flags are kept inline: "snippet only" or "summary" means the figure came from a search digest and the primary text was not opened; "secondary" means a later paper quoting the original; "unverified recollection" means a researcher's memory with no source retrieved; "researcher's arithmetic" means a calculation on sourced numbers. Design proposals, including every example motif, are untested.

## Ocarina's five notes work because contour, rhythm and bass do the differentiating

### Evidence: one small set, recoloured by tonal centre and phrase shape

Koji Kondo is quoted, in a fan-translated interview reproduced by a secondary article, as saying he "had only 5 notes to use for the ocarina tunes (because of button limitations)", which made composing twelve melodies in different moods difficult ([Zelda Universe](https://zeldauniverse.net/features/the-brilliance-of-koji-kondos-sound-design-in-ocarina-of-time/)); the wording is consistent across sources but the interview date given there is doubtful. The five notes are **D, F, A, B and the D an octave higher**, a D minor triad plus the major sixth; the same article admits the reason for that choice is conjecture, and no primary statement from Kondo explaining it was found. Two details conflict between sources: the octave (D4 to D5 as usually quoted, D5 to D6 per a Zelda Wiki search extract) and the song count (12 learned songs, or 13 with the player-composed Scarecrow's Song) ([FlyingOmelette FAQ](https://www.flyingomelette.com/faqs/z64/z64songs.html)).

Analysts, not Kondo, explain the variety in two ways. First, the same notes are centred on different pitches, so Song of Time reads as D Dorian and Saria's Song as F Lydian, and the game answers the player's short input with a full harmonised tune that is free to leave the five notes ([ThinkSpace Education](https://thinkspace.ac.uk/blog/a-lesson-in-modes-from-the-legend-of-zelda/)). Second, the six main songs share a form: **two short phrases on a three-note motif, then a longer answering phrase that moves in the opposite direction** ([Zelda Universe](https://zeldauniverse.net/features/the-brilliance-of-koji-kondos-sound-design-in-ocarina-of-time/)). Both analyses are journalistic or educational, not peer reviewed. The researchers' recalled button sequences (for example Saria's Song as F, A, B played twice and Song of Time as A, D, F played twice, with longer non-repeating warp songs) fit that description but are **unverified recollection** and must be checked against a source such as the [Zelda Dungeon song list](https://zeldadungeon.net/wiki/Ocarina_of_Time_Songs) before being quoted. Ocarina also turns songs into owned tools that teleport and solve puzzles, and reprises a learned tune as the background music of its area ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Ocarina_of_Time)).

The cognitive literature explains why this works. Melodies are stored as a scale schema plus contour, and contour dominates immediate recognition of short novel melodies ([Dowling 1978](https://labs.utdallas.edu/mpac/files/2021/03/1978-2.pdf)); infants and untrained adults detect contour changes far more readily than interval changes, and young children reproduce a tune's contour correctly before its intervals ([Trainor et al. 2002](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/trainor_et_al_2002.pdf); [review, PMC2841133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2841133)). A study of 100 earworms against 100 matched tunes found they had **faster tempi, common overall contours and one unusual feature** in their pattern of leaps ([Goldsmiths](https://research.gold.ac.uk/19405)). Melody identity survives changes of key, tempo and timbre ([Schellenberg & Habashi 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergHabachi2015.pdf)).

### Proposal: borrow the form, drop the tritone

The Ocarina set contains one tritone (F to B), which gives it Dorian and Lydian colour (researcher's interval arithmetic). A set for small children can omit it at the cost of some spice and recover the colour from the accompaniment. The transferable rules are these: identity comes from first note, contour and rhythm; short songs are a three-note cell played twice so the child memorises three items, not six; the game answers a correct input with a richer arrangement; and a handful of songs become permanent, story-relevant possessions.

## Choose la, do, mi, so on fast-attack timbres and code every button redundantly

### Evidence: pentatonic material, wide spacing, sharp onsets, more than pitch

Children's music pedagogy converges on semitone-free material. Kodály practice starts from the so-mi minor third and adds la, do and re, delaying fa and ti because half steps are hard for children ([Midwestern State University](https://msutexas.edu/academics/finearts/music/kodaly/what-is.php)); Orff Schulwerk begins with pentatonic melodies over an open-fifth drone whose missing third "enforces a non-functional harmony" ([Wikipedia: Orff Schulwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orff_Schulwerk)); Gordon's sequence starts with tonic and dominant patterns that are mostly arpeggiated rather than stepwise ([GIML](https://giml.org/mlt/lsa-tonalcontent/)). These orders rest on tradition and expert consensus; no controlled comparison of one order against another was found. Ralph Baer applied the same logic to Simon, choosing four bugle-like tones of an A major triad so the game is "always harmonic... no matter the sequence", after judging the earlier Touch Me to have "miserable, rasping sounds" ([Wikipedia: Simon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_(game))).

Hearing the difference between notes is not the bottleneck; naming direction is. Children aged 6 to 15 discriminate pitch differences of about **0.17 semitones** on average ([Deroche et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3339501)), but 5- and 6-year-olds do not typically apply "higher" or "lower" to pitch without targeted training ([Saindon et al. 2016](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SaindonEtAl2016.pdf)), and direction judgement improves from 5 to about 8 (secondary; the per-interval figures could not be read). Height-pitch mapping was unreliable in sighted Dutch children until about 11, while thickness-pitch (thick is low, thin is high) appeared earlier and more robustly ([Oxford record](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publication/1299933)). For blind listeners the picture is incomplete: early-blind adults showed a pitch-to-vertical-space association comparable to sighted adults ([Cattaneo et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-018-1495-x), from a search extract), another study found no pitch-direction association in blind participants for tactile movement ([Deroy et al. 2016](https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/6569/1/OD-ET-AL-JEP-16.pdf)), and **no study of blind children was found**.

Pitch alone is also the weakest way to label a button. In adult studies, abstract musical cues (earcons) were "much more difficult to learn" than sped-up speech, sung labels (lyricons) and spearcons were learned and recalled best, and meaningful sounds beat abstract ones ([Georgia Tech](https://repository.gatech.edu/entities/publication/8cf42420-1aaf-4a59-9ccb-7c7458346a9d); [Virginia Tech](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/items/b228d90f-5d4c-4d8b-ad4b-0cfab60e4c90); summaries of abstracts, adults only). Accessibility guidelines ask for a distinct sound per key event and a mono option ([Xbox Accessibility Guidelines](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/105); [AFB](https://afb.org/aw/winter2024/accessibility-gaming-deaf-blind)). Left/right hearing is close to adult-like from about 5 in free field and 8 to 10 over headphones (summary, sighted children) ([Frontiers 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnsys.2020.00039/pdf)).

Timbre decides how precisely a rhythm can be heard. A sound's perceived beat location depends mainly on attack and duration: fast-attack, short sounds are heard close to their onset with a narrow spread, slow-attack, long sounds late and vaguely ([Danielsen et al. 2019](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/63979?show=full)); a slowly bowed note can be heard **50 to 100 ms after its physical onset** ([Wikipedia: Perceptual attack time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptual_attack_time)). Children aged 9 to 11 remember sung melodies better than instrumental ones ([Weiss et al. 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/WeissEtAl2015.pdf)), and 5- to 9-year-olds lean more on absolute pitch level than older listeners ([Stalinski & Schellenberg 2010](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/StalinskiSchellenberg2010.pdf)). That last point matters more for this audience: 12 of 21 musically trained early-blind adults reported absolute pitch ([Hamilton et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15073518/)), and an informal 1980s sample put it at 40% of blind children ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/?p=32692)); both samples are small or selected.

### Proposal: one fixed set, voiced in thirds

The researchers compared candidate sets from first principles (their own interval arithmetic; no source ranks four-note sets for children).

| Set, low to high | Gaps in semitones | Worst interval inside the set | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| do re mi fa | 2, 2, 1 | Semitone | Avoid: hardest to tell apart, clashes with most chords |
| re fa la ti (Ocarina minus one) | 3, 4, 2 | Tritone | Colourful, but risky for small children |
| do re mi so | 2, 2, 3 | Major second | Singable; two whole-tone neighbours are confusable |
| do mi so la | 4, 3, 2 | Major second | Major and relative minor in one set; one whole-tone neighbour |
| do mi so do' | 4, 3, 5 | None | Most separable, but only one chord; needs rhythm for variety |
| **la do mi so** (proposed) | **3, 4, 3** | **None adjacent; a minor seventh at the extremes** | Same pitch classes as do mi so la, no whole-tone neighbour |

The recommended set is **la, do, mi, so with la at the bottom**, for example **D4, F4, A4, C5** (a subset of F major pentatonic, equally D minor pentatonic). This voicing is this report's own arithmetic on the researchers' two favourites: it keeps the major/minor ambiguity of do-mi-so-la (the notes read as F6 over an F bass and Dm7 over a D bass) while giving every neighbouring pair the minor-third-or-wider spacing the researchers wanted for 5- to 7-year-olds. It is also the Ocarina's D minor triad with the tritone-making B swapped for C. Buttons 4 and 3 are the so-mi third that Kodály practice starts from, and buttons 2, 3, 4 are the tonic arpeggio Gordon's sequence starts from. Use 12-tone equal temperament and one global key; the researchers judge temperament differences (about 14 to 16 cents) irrelevant next to 300 to 700 cent gaps, with no evidence either way in children.

Keep the button-to-pitch map and the absolute pitches identical across every instrument and every session, with octave shifts as the only allowed change. A fixed map lets a song learned on one instrument be replayed on another, respects young children's reliance on absolute pitch, and avoids disorienting the absolute-pitch listeners who are over-represented among blind musicians. Darken a song by moving the bass, not by transposing the buttons.

Give each button four redundant identities during learning and fade them as difficulty steps: its pitch; a sung syllable ("la", "do", "mi", "so", all consonant-initial, which keeps the perceived onset near the vowel); a stereo position grouped as left pair and right pair to match the physical layout; and a haptic signature. The thickness-pitch finding suggests rounder, bigger timbre and a stronger, lower rumble for low notes and a thinner sound and lighter tick for high ones. Hard left/right grouping is supported from about age 5; four distinct pan positions are not supported by any child data found. Stereo requires a start-of-session "which ear?" check and a mono fallback in which pitch and timbre alone still work.

For sound, default to struck or plucked timbres with natural decay: marimba, kalimba, harp or pizzicato, steel pan, with glockenspiel for sparkle (this shortlist is the researcher's inference from the attack-time evidence). Slow-attack sounds such as flute or choir need a short percussive transient layered on the onset, and each instrument needs its own judging offset so a child copying what was heard is not marked late; the per-instrument offsets could not be extracted from the papers and must be measured. Place playable notes between roughly C4 and C6 and keep drones an octave or more below; the register advice is engineering judgement, not a cited finding. Children's headphone exposure should respect the WHO figure of 75 dB(A) for sensitive users, which many "volume-limited" children's headphones do not meet ([PMC6928536](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6928536)).

The accompaniment should be safe at three levels (researcher's inference from the Orff model and chord arithmetic). During the child's turn, thin it to drums plus an open-fifth drone on do and so, over which every button is consonant at any moment. For mood, move the bass among do (bright, F in the example key), la (minor, the "battle" colour, D) and fa (warm, B flat), and voice any dominant chord without its third, because do against the third of V is the set's one regular clash. Do not adopt Wii Music's substitution, in which the game picks the pitch for each gesture ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/nintendo-wii-music-anniversary-15-years)): it guarantees consonance but destroys the fixed button-to-pitch memory the game exists to build.

## Battle songs are short cells that the call itself teaches

### Evidence: memory span, rhythm development and cueing without a screen

Span sets the length. Mean verbal short-term span is about 4.5 items at ages 4 to 6, 5 at 7 to 8 and 6 at 9 to 11 (factsheet; column headings unverified) ([University of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/media-library/sites/expsych/documents/factsheet-2.pdf)), and memory for tone sequences matures later, between about 6 and 13 (secondary) ([Ginzburg et al. 2022](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Ginzburg_et_al._2022.pdf)). In a four-button Simon-type task with 374 children, span rose with age, **regular, chunkable sequences were retained better at every age**, and raw span resisted practice, so gains come from structure, not drilling ([Mathy et al. 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4763062/)). Children aged 8 to 9 retained a repeating sequence over a delay better than adults ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29250874/)). Blind children probably start ahead here: blind 10-year-olds outperformed sighted peers on forward and backward digit span ([Withagen et al. 2013](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/short-term-memory-and-working-memory-in-blind-versus-sighted-chil/)), although one study of 19 children found no advantage ([Wyver & Markham](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/do-children-with-visual-impairments-demonstrate-superior-short-te/)).

Rhythm has its own developmental order. Children aged 5 and 7 organise rhythms around an internal pulse, and "the closer rhythms are to a regular beat, the easier they are to reproduce" (abstract only) ([Drake & Gérard 1989](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF00309271)); listeners sort durations into a few classes, typically in 1:2 ratio, and binary subdivision is easier than ternary (abstract and secondary) ([Drake 1993](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03211712.pdf)). Gordon's rhythm sequence runs duple before triple, and beat and division patterns before elongations, rests and ties ([GIML](https://giml.org/mlt/lsa-rhythmcontent/)). That syncopation and rests are late skills, around 8 and above, is a researcher's background recollection, not sourced.

Speed is counter-intuitive. Young children's spontaneous tapping interval is about **400 to 500 ms** (120 to 150 BPM) and is reported stable to about age 7 (secondary) ([Bobin-Bègue et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01346/full)); measured values at ages 7 and 9 average about 115 BPM with a large spread between children ([Kertész & Honbolygó 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9911382)). Five-year-olds asked to tap very slowly could not hold intervals much beyond 1.5 s. Slow is therefore harder, not easier. One digest gives a faster preferred tempo for under-6s (150 to 200 BPM); the sources were not reconciled.

Mainstream games show how to notate by sound. Rhythm Heaven, proposed by Tsunku as a rhythm game without visual indicators and relying "mainly on audio cues", lets players practise each pattern before the scored attempt, and its designers insist that hard stages sometimes have "fewer notes or a slower tempo" and that rhythms sit "where they make sense" ([Wikipedia: Rhythm Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_Heaven); [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/rhythmheavenfever/0/0)). Tsunku had every voice cue re-recorded because "it doesn't have bounce" ([Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/rhythmheavenfever/0/4)). In Space Channel 5 the opponents' vocal calls name the inputs ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Channel_5)); Patapon gives each of four buttons a drum syllable and builds four-beat commands from them (snippet only) ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/patapon-review/)). A racing display for blind players uses four evenly spaced beeps whose rhythm lets players anticipate the exact moment to act ([RAD project](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~brian/projects/rad.html)). In a small case study, students made fewer errors when they spoke a rhythm before playing it ([Fust 2006](https://ir.library.louisville.edu/etd/473)); evidence that any particular syllable system helps is otherwise weak.

### Proposal: composition rules for a four-note songbook

Write every song as cells. A cell is two to four notes inside one four-beat bar; a short song is a cell played twice; a long song is cell, cell, answer, with the answer moving in the opposite direction, as in Ocarina. Give every song a unique pairing of first note and three-note contour, and a unique rhythm cell; two songs may share pitches or rhythm, never both. Put exactly one surprise in each motif (a leap, a repeated note, one off-beat) on an otherwise ordinary shape. End question phrases on so or mi and answers on do, or on la for minor songs. Build rhythm from two duration classes in 1:2 ratio that stay on the pulse, and treat a rest or an off-beat as a new concept that gets its own practice round. Make songs harder by adding structure, not speed: keep the pulse between about 100 and 130 BPM for the youngest and never slow a song down to make it easier.

Let the call be the notation. The enemy or teacher performs the phrase in a timbre and register distinct from the backing, the first exposures are sung on the note names or chanted on a word whose syllables match the rhythm, and the labels fade as the child succeeds. The researchers suggest word rhythms for the first levels (in Italian, "me-la" for two even notes, "fra-go-la" for three, "co-co-me-ro" for four; in English "ap-ple", "straw-ber-ry", "wa-ter-mel-on"); these are untested suggestions and each word's stress must be checked against the intended accent. Voice lines must be recorded in strict time. Separating the cue from the backing by register, timbre and ducking is inference from general auditory principles; no source was retrieved.

The motifs below illustrate the rules on the proposed set (button 1 = la, 2 = do, 3 = mi, 4 = so). In the rhythm column each symbol is one eighth note, L, D, M, S mark an onset, a dash holds or rests, and a slash is a bar line; "Lullaby" is in 3/4 with one symbol per quarter note. All are illustrations, adapted by this report from the researchers' examples, and none has been played to a child.

| Motif | Buttons | Rhythm grid | Shape and hook | Suggested use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hello | 4 3 4 4 3 | S – M – S S M – | Falling third, the playground chant | First tutorial, two buttons only |
| Rise | 2 3 4, twice | D M S – D M S – | Up-up arpeggio, short-short-long | First three-button song; "open" or "wake" spell |
| Fall | 4 3 2 1 | S – M D L – – – | Down all the way, long-short-short-long | Shield or sleep; ends on la |
| Hop | 1 4 3, twice | L – S – M – – – | One big leap up, step down | Introduces the widest leap |
| Gallop | 3 3 4 3 3 2 | M M S – M M D – | Repeated notes; identity is the rhythm | Fast enemy; trains repeated presses |
| Question | 2 2 3 4 | D – D – M – S – | Climbs and stops on so (open) | Enemy call that begs an answer |
| Answer | 3 4 2 | M – S – D – – – | Up, then drop home to do (closed) | Child's reply in answer mode |
| Battle | 1 1 2 3 2 1 | L L D – M – – D / L – – – – – – – | Minor colour over a la bass, one off-beat | Boss phrase for ages 8 and up |
| Lullaby | 3 2 3 4 3 2 1 | M – D / M – S / M – D / L – – | Rocking thirds in 3/4 | First triple-metre song; calming scene |

The cheapest multiplier is rhythm alone: do-mi-so as three even quarters, as long-short-short, and as short-short-long are three different songs that train exactly the skill the game is about. With four buttons there are 36 three-note cells without immediate repeats (researcher's count), so pitch never runs out.

## Six instruments share one pitch map but ask for different gestures

### Evidence: buttons for dense rhythm, motion for slow expression, vibration as support

Nintendo prototyped swinging the Wii Remote for Rhythm Heaven Fever and dropped it because it "made our hands tired" and the series needs very many inputs per minute; the DS flick gesture took months and worked only once paired with a sound ([Iwata Asks: Fever](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/rhythmheavenfever/0/0); [Iwata Asks: DS](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/ds/rhythm-heaven/0/2)). Zelda's motion and microphone instruments drew complaints of being unresponsive or of asking nothing but waggling (snippet only) ([Zelda Dungeon](https://www.zeldadungeon.net/?p=81763)). Wii Music shows the other side: gesture families mapped to instrument types are enjoyable when nothing can fail, though reviewers expected older children to tire of goal-free play within hours ([Wikipedia: Wii Music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wii_Music)). For this audience specifically, visually impaired children asked to invent controls preferred buttons and proposed only shaking as a motion gesture (summary) ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145%2F3490149.3501327)); 13 blind children could play a motion tennis game when audio and vibration told them when to swing (press release) ([University of Nevada, Reno](https://www.unr.edu/nevada-today/news/2010/video-game-accessibility-project-to-help-blind-children-exercise)); and continuous closed-loop sound let a blind adult steer a racing car precisely ([RAD project](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~brian/projects/rad.html)).

Haptics have hard limits. Eccentric-mass motors take on the order of **50 to 100 ms to start and stop** (vendor article) ([Power Electronic Tips](https://www.powerelectronictips.com/haptics-components-pt-1-lra-erm-and-piezo-actuators/)). Blind Hero, which cued Guitar Hero through one pager motor per finger, had to start each vibration about a quarter of a second early, removed sustained notes because duration was hard to judge by touch, and reached 15 to 50% accuracy against 60 to 90% for sighted play of the visual game (secondary review page; sample sizes not seen) ([review](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69dc0c11502ad1a83c7ff006)). Rhythm is nonetheless the best-recognised vibration parameter, at 93% in adult tests with a laboratory actuator (summary) ([Brown et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/1393758.pdf)). Adults can synchronise to a vibrating beat, less well than to sound, and adding touch to sound helps inconsistently ([Tranchant et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5601036/); [Ammirante et al. 2016](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13423-016-1067-9)). In a non-visual Simon game, sequences were replicated better with haptics plus sound than with haptics alone (abstract, adults) ([Kuber et al.](https://mdsoar.org/handle/11603/19922)). There is no haptic evidence from children.

### Proposal: a starter voice, a default drum and four gesture instruments

All six instruments below use the same four pitches on the same buttons; what changes is the gesture, what is judged and how the motors behave. Because the IMU's yaw drifts, no instrument uses absolute heading: tilt uses pitch and roll, which are absolute, and sweeps use rate of change only. Whether shake and flick can be detected reliably from the current HID report, and with what latency, is unmeasured.

| Instrument | How it is played | What is judged | Haptics | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Voice (sung la, do, mi, so) | Tap a button | Order first, then rhythm | Soft tick on each press | Starter instrument; teaches button names by ear |
| Drum or marimba | Tap a button | Order and rhythm | Short sharp tick; extra kick on a perfect hit | Default for most battles; highest precision |
| Flute | Hold a button for the note's length; tilt up for the upper octave | Order plus short versus long only | Gentle continuous hum while held | Introduces duration; slow, lyrical enemies |
| Shaker | Shake during a marked window | Presence or rough count, never per-shake timing | Rattle that grows with the shaking | "Charge up" between phrases; short bursts only |
| Bell | Hold a button, then one firm downward flick | One timed event per bar, wide window | Single strong pulse | Finishing move; one or two per battle |
| Harp | Hold a button to choose the note, roll the controller to strum | Number and direction of strums, not exact timing | Light tick per string plucked | Spell moments and puzzles outside combat |

The Voice and Drum carry the game; the rest are variety that reuses the same memory. The Flute is where duration enters, late and with only two classes, since duration was the first thing Blind Hero cut. The Shaker and Bell follow the evidence that motion should be coarse, infrequent and immediately audible, with thresholds and hysteresis to ignore unintentional movement. The Harp must sound one string per increment of roll so that it is not empty waggling. Tilt-for-octave doubles the range to eight pitches while preserving the button-to-degree map; tilting up for higher matches the adult association that survives early blindness, but must be tested with the children.

The two motors take three jobs in every instrument, in this order of priority: a steady beat pulse during listening and early attempts, switched on automatically after failures and faded with success; a brief confirmation of each press; and phrase-level reward as a long roll. Meaning should live in rhythm and in which motor fires, not in fine intensity or duration differences. If the motors are eccentric-mass types they can mark beats at 120 BPM but not fast subdivisions, and cue pulses must be triggered early by a measured lead; the motor type and its position in the controller are not stated in the brief. Vibration never identifies which of four buttons to press on its own.

## The battle loop alternates listen and echo over a beat that never stops

### Evidence: what proven loops and measured children's timing say

The canonical loop is strict alternation. In PaRappa the teacher raps a line and the player answers; ranks move only after two consecutive good or bad lines, and one bad line merely flashes a warning ([Wikipedia: PaRappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PaRappa_the_Rapper)). Patapon alternates four beats of player command with four beats of the tribe singing it back, and sustained success opens an audible "Fever" state (loop description snippet only) ([Wikipedia: Patapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patapon)). Rhythm Heaven shows only three end-of-stage verdicts and offers a skip after three consecutive failures ([Wikipedia: Rhythm Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_Heaven)). Simon and Loom are the non-metric variant, judging order only; Loom starts with three notes and unlocks more through the story ([Wikipedia: Loom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loom_(video_game))). That the backing track continues through the player's turn in PaRappa and Space Channel 5 is unverified recollection.

Feedback should reward, not punish. Hi-Fi Rush registers off-beat presses and still makes them land musically, on the director's principle that "the worst thing you can do in an action game is to press the button and nothing happens" ([Tacoma Ledger](https://thetacomaledger.com/2023/02/27/the-ingenious-accessibility-of-hi-fi-rush/)). Crypt of the NecroDancer widened its timing window after a strict one caused stress errors and added auto-calibration for players who run early or late ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crypt_of_the_NecroDancer)). Thumper gives muted sound for adequate timing and a bright ping plus controller shake for exact timing, but cuts the music and plays harsh noise on a miss, which its reviewer says often causes a second mistake ([SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/the-helpful-harmful-sounds-of-thumper/)). Mother 3 makes the hidden beat audible as a heartbeat when the player needs help (snippet only) ([Wikipedia: Mother 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mother_3)).

Children's timing bounds the windows. At age 7, mean asynchrony to a metronome was 0.13 to 0.16 of the beat interval; at age 9 it fell to 0.09 at 120 BPM and 0.07 at 150 BPM but not at 80 BPM ([Kertész & Honbolygó 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9911382)). By the researcher's arithmetic that is roughly **95, 80 and 60 ms at age 7 and 125, 45 and 26 ms at age 9** for 80, 120 and 150 BPM. Four- and five-year-olds are reported not to synchronise well, if at all, with 6- and 7-year-olds much better (snippet only) ([Repp review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-012-0371-2)), and the adult habit of tapping slightly ahead of the beat is rare in young children. Young children synchronised better with a human partner than with a machine, at ages 2.5 to 4.5 (abstract plus secondary) ([Kirschner & Tomasello 2009](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458233)). For comparison, the most lenient osu!taiko setting accepts ±50 ms as "great" and ±120 ms as "OK" ([osu! wiki](https://osu.ppy.sh/wiki/en/Gameplay/Judgement/osu%21taiko)), and Mother 3's default is ±1 and ±3 frames, about ±17 and ±50 ms by the researcher's conversion ([Data Crystal](https://datacrystal.tcrf.net/wiki/Mother_3/Sound_battle_timing_windows)). Windows for Rhythm Heaven, Guitar Hero, arcade Taiko and DDR could not be sourced and are left out; a StepMania range of 22.5 to 180 ms comes from a low-reliability blog.

Latency is the dominant engineering risk. The controller's radio link is fast (HID over BLE allows a 7.5 ms connection interval) ([TI](https://www.ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/SWRA715/GUID-3E3206EB-BACF-4F54-AEFE-0C98C5B25389)), but a single forum report measured about 80 ms for a BLE HID device on Windows against 1.5 ms on another host ([Nordic DevZone](https://devzone.nordicsemi.com/f/nordic-q-a/119571/unusually-high-ble-connection-latency/526410)). Bluetooth headphones add **113 to 241 ms** on the common SBC codec, against 7 ms wired ([RTINGS](https://www.rtings.com/headphones/learn/latency)). Non-musician adults notice a press-to-sound delay only at about 180 ± 104 ms (summary; no child data) ([van Vugt & Tillmann](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3911931/)). A tap-along test measures audio plus input latency together, should use silent taps, and conventionally takes about ten taps and drops the extremes ([Rhythm Quest devlog](https://rhythmquestgame.com/devlog/10.html)). Unity's `AudioSource.PlayScheduled` starts clips at an absolute time on the audio clock, and Unity recommends scheduling 100 to 200 ms ahead ([Unity documentation](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/current/script-reference/unityengine/audiosource/playscheduled)).

Onboarding and difficulty have a few firm findings. In a usability study of ten mobile audio games, the missing audio tutorial and missing difficulty settings were the main failings (summary) ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-58703-5_18)); guidance asks for a tutorial replayable at any time ([Can I Play That](https://caniplaythat.com/basic-accessibility-options-for-blind-and-low-vision-players/)). Blind Hero players needed the first minutes just to adapt to the device, then began playing from memory rather than from cues, plateauing after about 30 minutes. Controller difficulty was a suspected source of competence frustration in children aged 10 to 13 ([Tampere thesis](https://trepo.tuni.fi/handle/10024/205279)). Around **half of children with vision impairment have additional needs** by RNIB's working figure ([RNIB](https://www.rnib.org.uk/knowledge-and-research-hub/research-reports/education-research/educational-provision)), and visually impaired children show motor-skill differences from sighted peers ([Ebrahimi et al. 2024](https://doaj.org/article/78fda264f69a4bdd91acd0363951c429)). One controlled experiment outside rhythm games found dynamic difficulty adjustment raised performance but not enjoyment (snippet only) ([Acta Ludologica](https://actaludologica.com/performance-based-dynamic-difficulty-adjustment-and-player-experience-in-a-2d-digital-game-a-controlled-experiment/)). Session-length rules, an 80 to 85% target success rate and "no lives under 8" are background recollection without sources.

### Proposal: phases of a battle

A battle is a conversation in four-beat bars over a drum-and-drone backing that starts before the first call and never stops, because continuing a beat without a pacing sound is where young children are weakest. It opens with the enemy's signature cue and name and a fixed four-beat count-in. Then come **defence rounds**: the enemy plays a one-bar call in its own timbre, a constant "your turn" sound marks the last beat, and the child echoes in the next bar on their chosen instrument. Each round is judged as a whole phrase. On success a companion sings the phrase back and an accompaniment layer joins; the next call extends the song cumulatively (cell, then cell twice, then cell, cell, answer), which exploits repetition learning. After three or four rounds comes an **attack round** with lower memory load and more agency: the child plays one of their owned spell songs from the long-term songbook, or answers the enemy's Question with any phrase that ends on do. A **finisher** is a single gesture on a slow instrument, such as one bell flick on the count. Victory replays the whole song fully harmonised with the enemy's theme joining the child's, the Ocarina reward. Later bosses recombine earlier enemies' phrases, as Rhythm Heaven's remixes do, and a phrase played backwards can serve as a counter-spell, as in Loom.

Failure is staged and never silent. A wrong button still sounds its own consonant note; a missed note is simply absent; the groove continues. The first failed phrase produces a funny enemy reaction and the same call again. The second adds support: sung note names return and the beat pulses in the motors. The third consecutive failure shortens the phrase to its first cell; after three failed attempts at the battle the companion offers to help or move on, told as story. Only the top music layer is lost on a failed phrase. Early-or-late coaching belongs in practice, not battles, and feedback should distinguish "wrong button" from "right buttons, wobbly rhythm".

### Proposal: timing judgement in three tiers

The first tier judges **order only**: the right buttons in the right order at any speed, as Simon and Ocarina do. The second tier, the default, judges **relative rhythm**: the child's first press defines their own beat one, the gaps between presses are scaled to the phrase's total length, and each gap passes if it is nearer to its own duration class than to the neighbouring one (a short must be clearly shorter than a long). This uses the finding that listeners sort durations into a few classes, needs no calibration, and cancels any constant latency because a fixed delay shifts every onset equally. The classification rule itself is this report's suggestion and is untested. The third tier judges **absolute synchrony** against the backing for play-along moments and older children, on the audio clock minus a calibrated offset, with starting windows of about **±150 ms for ages 5 to 7 and ±100 ms for ages 8 to 12**, an inner "perfect" band of ±50 to 60 ms that only adds sparkle, and symmetric or late-forgiving scoring. At eighth notes around 100 BPM a ±150 ms window already covers the whole timeline, so beyond that density only order and count can be judged. Store a per-child window multiplier and adapt it slowly; given how wide individual differences are in both blind players and children, per-child tuning matters more than the age label.

### Proposal: progression by age and stage

Age bands are entry points; a child moves up as soon as two or three phrases in a row succeed, and blind children may climb faster than these sighted-norm values suggest.

| Stage | Typical entry age | Buttons | Phrase length | Rhythm content | Pulse | Judgement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Cause and effect | Below 5, or additional needs | 1 to 4, free | None | Any press makes a pleasant sound | None | None; cannot fail |
| 1. Echo by order | 5 to 6 | 2 (so, mi), then 3 | 2 to 3 notes | Even notes on the beat | Child's own tempo, about 110 to 130 BPM | Order only |
| 2. Echo with rhythm | 6 to 8 | 3, then 4 | 3 to 4 notes | Two durations in 1:2 ratio | 100 to 130 BPM | Relative rhythm |
| 3. Songs | 8 to 10 | 4 | 4 to 5 notes, cell twice | Adds one rest, then one off-beat | 90 to 130 BPM | Relative, then absolute at ±100 ms |
| 4. Mastery | 10 to 12 | 4 plus tilt octave | 5 to 8 notes, cell, cell, answer | Triple metre, syncopation, held notes | 80 to 140 BPM | Absolute; optional "perfect" challenges |

Add difficulty in this order: number of buttons, phrase length, rhythmic variety, and tempo last. Offer an adult-settable free-rhythm mode and a no-fail mode, but keep goals (collecting songs, story progress) in them, since goal-free music play bored older children in Wii Music's reviews. An audio songbook that replays any learned song slowly on request stands in for a written list and doubles as the collection screen. Do not promise reading or attention benefits; the transfer evidence is positive in dyslexia and otherwise inconclusive.

### Proposal: latency handling and onboarding

Drive everything from the audio clock: schedule calls, count-ins and backing loops with `PlayScheduled` at least 100 to 200 ms ahead and compute beats from `dspTime`, never frame time. Recommend wired headphones or speakers; on that path the press-to-sound budget is estimated under about 50 ms (researcher's arithmetic), so sounding the note immediately on press is sound. If calibration reveals a large offset, as with Bluetooth headphones, stay in the relative-rhythm tier, where the offset does not matter, and for play-along moments pre-schedule the correct note and merely soften it when no press arrives. Measure three things on the real hardware before fixing any window: BLE HID latency and jitter on each target operating system (especially Windows), motor rise and fall time, and gesture-detection delay.

Onboarding is a story-framed ladder with no visuals. It begins with free play in which every button sings its name and nothing can fail, which covers the device-adaptation minutes. A "which ear?" check follows if stereo is used. A free-tapping warm-up ("drum however you like") measures the child's own tempo and centres the first levels on it. A disguised calibration ("tap along with the drum eight times", taps silent, extremes dropped) sets the offset and can be run by an adult once per setup. Then the game teaches so and mi with "Hello", adds do, then la, one button at a time, always in say-then-play form with the narrator as a partner who plays along before fading out. The tutorial stays replayable through a dedicated long press.

## What only playtesting with blind children can settle

The most consequential unknown is the note set itself: whether la-do-mi-so in thirds is easier to identify among four alternatives than do-mi-so-la or the bugle-call do-mi-so-do', and whether the minor-seventh span from button 1 to button 4 sounds awkward in leaps. No data exist on four-alternative pitch identification by age, so this needs a direct comparison with children at 5 to 7 and at 8 to 12. Closely related is how fast the redundant labels can fade: whether sung note names help or distract, whether the left-pair/right-pair pan is noticed at all, and whether absolute-pitch listeners are bothered by the octave shift.

Timing needs its own measurements, because no synchronisation data for blind children were found. Record the spread of press timing per age band on the actual controller, compare the three judging tiers for how fair they feel, and test whether the relative-rhythm rule passes performances an adult listener would call correct. Check the assumed tempo range, since the sources disagree on young children's preferred tempo, and verify that the youngest children can enter on the bar after the handover cue or need the game to wait for their first press.

Haptics and motion are almost entirely unevidenced for children. Test whether a vibrating beat steadies or distracts, whether children can tell the two motors apart in the hand, how much lead the motors need, how long shaking stays fun before it tires, and whether tilt-up for the higher octave is intuitive without sight. Measure whether hold duration on the Flute can be judged fairly once the release is heard rather than felt.

Finally, the human factors: phrase-length ceilings per age when timing must be reproduced as well as order; how many owned songs a child retains across days; session length before fatigue; headphone tolerance versus speakers; the right floor for children with additional needs; and whether the three-failure helper feels like kindness or like losing. Two prior works should be read in full first, because they are the only rhythm games tested with or built for this audience and their texts were unreachable: the Utrecht BongoBeats theses on rhythm play with visually impaired children ([record](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/43216)) and the Feel the Rhythm thesis on audio-plus-vibration rhythm cueing ([record](https://repository.lib.umassd.edu/esploro/outputs/graduate/Feel-the-rhythm-a-blind-accessible-rhythm/9914424803801301)).

## Conclusion

The research reverses two instincts. The first is that a four-button limit is a musical handicap to be worked around; the evidence says it is the design. A fixed, consonant set makes wrong answers pleasant, makes the accompaniment free to change the mood, and puts the game's whole identity into contour and rhythm, which are exactly what young listeners remember. The second is that accessibility means simplifying a sighted rhythm game. The stronger position is that the call is the notation: a sung, rhythmically exact phrase tells a blind child what to press and when more completely than a scrolling chart tells a sighted one, and judging the echo by its internal rhythm removes the latency problem that plagues every wireless rhythm game.

What remains is empirical, and narrow enough to settle quickly. A two-button "Hello" prototype with the Voice and Drum instruments, the three judging tiers switchable, and logging of raw press times would answer the note-spacing, tempo and window questions in a handful of sessions with children, and would produce the first timing data for blind children that this literature search could not find.
