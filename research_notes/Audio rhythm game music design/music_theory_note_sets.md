## 1. Ocarina of Time: which notes, how the songs differ, why the set works

### Takeaway
Kondo is on record that button limits gave him only 5 notes for 12 ocarina melodies and that this made composing hard; the set is D, F, A, B, D' (a D minor triad plus the major sixth and the octave). Analysts explain the variety by (a) shifting the tonal centre so the same notes read as D Dorian, F Lydian, etc., and (b) a shared "two short phrases built on a 3-note motif, then a longer answer" form in which contour and rhythm carry the identity.

### Cited Findings
- Kondo (quoted from a GlitterBerri-translated interview): "In all, there are 12 ocarina melodies, each corresponding with a theme." "I had only 5 notes to use for the ocarina tunes (because of button limitations)," ... "be they upbeat major or sad minor songs, making composing difficult." — [Zelda Universe, "The brilliance of Koji Kondo's sound design in Ocarina of Time", 2022](https://zeldauniverse.net/features/the-brilliance-of-koji-kondos-sound-design-in-ocarina-of-time/). Caveat: this is a secondary article quoting a fan translation; the article dates the interview "2021", which is doubtful (the same quote is hosted as an OoT-era development interview at [Zelda Dungeon Wiki](https://www.zeldadungeon.net/wiki/Interview:Ocarina_of_Time_Development_-_Playing_the_Ocarina), which I could not open). Treat wording as reliable, date as unconfirmed.
- The five notes are D, F, A, B and D an octave higher; the article describes them as forming a D minor 6th chord and admits the reason for the choice is conjecture ("we could only imagine"). — [Zelda Universe](https://zeldauniverse.net/features/the-brilliance-of-koji-kondos-sound-design-in-ocarina-of-time/)
- Octave conflict: Zelda Wiki lists the button pitches as D5, F5, A5, B5, D6 (per search-result extract), one octave above the D4 to D5 usually quoted; the wiki also says the ocarina can produce chromatic notes between low B and high F using modifier buttons/stick. — [Zelda Wiki, Ocarina of Time (Item)](https://zeldawiki.wiki/wiki/Ocarina_of_Time_(Item)) (page blocked to direct fetch; from search extract)
- Song count conflict: 12 learned songs plus the player-composed Scarecrow's Song (an 8-note song the player invents and must remember), so sources say 12 or 13. — [FlyingOmelette song FAQ](https://www.flyingomelette.com/faqs/z64/z64songs.html); [Zelda Dungeon Wiki](https://zeldadungeon.net/wiki/Ocarina_of_Time_Songs)
- Structural analysis (fan/journalistic, not peer reviewed): the six "main" songs (Zelda's Lullaby, Epona's Song, Saria's Song, Sun's Song, Song of Time, Song of Storms) all use a repeated three-note motif; each opens with two short phrases on that motif and resolves with a longer phrase; rising short phrases (Song of Storms, Saria's Song) are answered by a falling long phrase, and falling short phrases (Song of Time, Zelda's Lullaby) by a rising one. — [Zelda Universe](https://zeldauniverse.net/features/the-brilliance-of-koji-kondos-sound-design-in-ocarina-of-time/)
- Modal reinterpretation (educational blog from a film/game-music school): Song of Time is D Dorian, with B natural as the characteristic major sixth (D minor would have B flat); Serenade of Water is a D minor arpeggio that moves to B natural (Dorian) and the full tune ends on F sharp (Picardy third); Saria's Song is F Lydian, with B natural as the raised fourth over F. "By making the tunes centre around different notes, and therefore be in different modes, Koji Kondo has created great melodic diversity." — [ThinkSpace Education, "A Lesson in Modes from The Legend of Zelda"](https://thinkspace.ac.uk/blog/a-lesson-in-modes-from-the-legend-of-zelda/)
- The player only inputs the opening notes; the game then plays the full, harmonised tune automatically (which may include notes outside the five, such as the F sharp above). — [ThinkSpace Education](https://thinkspace.ac.uk/blog/a-lesson-in-modes-from-the-legend-of-zelda/)
- Kondo (1999 interview, translated) describes building the score around short, catchy motifs per area while avoiding repetition. — [shmuplations, Ocarina of Time developer interviews](https://shmuplations.com/ocarinaoftime/) (from search extract; page not opened)
- A student-journal harmonic analysis of Kondo's OoT score exists (Nota Bene, Univ. of Western Ontario, vol. 8 iss. 1). — [Nota Bene vol. 8 iss. 1 art. 4](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/notabene/vol8/iss1/4) (URL redirected; content not retrieved)

### Inferences
- UNVERIFIED (my recall; check against [Zelda Dungeon Wiki](https://zeldadungeon.net/wiki/Ocarina_of_Time_Songs) before publishing). Button map: A = D, C-down = F, C-right = A, C-left = B, C-up = D'. Input sequences as pitches:
  - Zelda's Lullaby: B D' A | B D' A
  - Epona's Song: D' B A | D' B A
  - Saria's Song: F A B | F A B
  - Sun's Song: A F D' | A F D'
  - Song of Time: A D F | A D F
  - Song of Storms: D F D' | D F D'
  - Minuet of Forest: D D' B A B A
  - Bolero of Fire: F D F D A F A F
  - Serenade of Water: D F A A B
  - Nocturne of Shadow: B A A D B A F
  - Requiem of Spirit: D F D A F D
  - Prelude of Light: D' A D' A B D'
- If that list is right, the design rules are visible: the 6 child songs are a 3-note cell played twice (so the player memorises 3 items, not 6); all six cells are different 3-note orderings with different first notes or contours (down-up, up-down, up-up, down-down...); the 6 warp songs are 5 to 8 notes, non-repeating, and each has a distinct rhythm (the names minuet, bolero, serenade, nocturne, requiem, prelude signal rhythmic character). Identity comes from first interval + contour + rhythm, not from the pitch set.
- Interval content of {D, F, A, B, D'} (my calculation): D-F m3, D-A P5, D-B M6, F-A M3, F-B tritone, A-B M2, plus octave. No semitone. One tritone (F-B) and one whole tone (A-B). Subsets: D-F-A = D minor; F-A-(D) also 6th of F; B-D-F = B diminished, with A = B half-diminished; D-F-A-B = Dm6 = Bm7b5 = G9 without root. Over a G bass the notes are 5, b7, 9, 3 of G7/G9 (G Mixolydian colour); over F they are 6, 1, 3, #4 (F Lydian); over D they are 1, b3, 5, 6 (D Dorian); over B flat or C the B natural clashes. So the set is "bass-agnostic" among D, F, G, (A minor, E minor partly) roots, which is what lets the accompaniment recolour the same button notes. This is standard chord arithmetic, not a quoted analysis.
- Lesson for EchoQuest: one tritone in the set is what gives OoT its modal spice (Dorian/Lydian); a children's 4-note set can omit it for safety, at the cost of less colour.

## 2. Properties of candidate 4-note sets

### Takeaway
Music pedagogy converges on semitone-free pentatonic material for young children (Kodály starts on the so-mi minor third, then adds la, do, re; Orff starts pentatonic over an open-fifth drone). Any 4-note subset of a major pentatonic scale has no semitone and no tritone, so every order and every simultaneous pair is at worst a mild major second.

### Cited Findings
- Kodály sequence: exercises begin with the minor third (mi-so) and add la, do and re one at a time; fa and ti come later because they create half steps that are hard for children to sing in tune; the pentatonic scale is used as a stepping stone. — [Kodály method (Wikipedia mirror)](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Kodály_Method); [Midwestern State Univ., "What is the Kodály Concept?"](https://msutexas.edu/academics/finearts/music/kodaly/what-is.php)
- The so-mi-first order is debated among teachers: some argue do should follow so-mi rather than la, and some start with do-re-mi; "Sol-Mi and Do-Re-Mi are both fine starting points". — [Dynamic Music Room](https://dynamicmusicroom.com/kodaly-method-first-interval); [Aula Virtual Música, "Why don't we start with DO?"](https://aulavirtualmusica.com/inicio/en/blog/why-dont-we-start-with-do) (practitioner blogs)
- Orff Schulwerk: music is largely modal, beginning with major and minor pentatonic scales; the drone or bordun (open fifth on degrees 1 and 5, no third) is the ground bass; hexachordal (added fa) and full seven-note material come later; "the drone bass enforces a non-functional harmony". — [Orff Schulwerk (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orff_Schulwerk)
- AOSA teacher-education sequence: improvise pentatonic melodies over drone accompaniments first, then hexatonic over drones, then modal melodies over drones and shifting triads. — [AOSA Recorder Level II curriculum](https://member.aosa.org/storage/files/shares/CMS%20Pages/TE%20Content/Curriculum%20Docs/Recorder_Level%20II_v4.5_Nov%202019.pdf)
- By about age 10 most students hear and internalise music with chord changes that fall outside the pentatonic. — [Music and the Child, ch. 4 (SUNY open textbook)](https://milnepublishing.geneseo.edu/music-and-the-child/chapter/chapter-4/)
- In musical systems the smallest structurally important pitch step tends to be at least one semitone. — [Stalinski & Schellenberg 2012, Topics in Cognitive Science](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/StalinskiSchellenberg2012TopiCS.pdf)

### Inferences
All interval arithmetic below is mine. Note names assume C = do; consonance over chords means "chord tone or mild added tone (2, 6, 9), no semitone clash with a chord tone".

| Set (solfège) | In C | Adjacent gaps (semitones) | Worst interval inside the set | Over I (C) | Over IV (F) | Over V (G) | Over vi (Am) | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| do re mi so | C4 D4 E4 G4 | 2, 2, 3 | M2 | ok (re = 9) | E is maj7 of F: soft, acceptable | C is the 4th against B of G: weak clash; D, G chord tones | ok | Stepwise, very singable; steps of 2 semitones are the smallest, hardest to tell apart |
| do mi so la | C4 E4 G4 A4 | 4, 3, 2 | M2 | ok (C6) | ok (A, C chord tones; E maj7, G 9) | C clashes mildly with B | all chord tones or b7 (Am7) | C6 = Am7: major/minor ambiguous, like OoT's Dm6 without the tritone |
| so la do re (mi) | G3 A3 C4 D4 (E4) | 2, 3, 2 | M2 | ok | ok | ok except C (sus4) | ok | Tonic in the middle; "plagal" folk-song range; good for cadence so-do |
| la do re mi (minor pentatonic) | A3 C4 D4 E4 | 3, 2, 2 | M2 | ok | ok | C clash | ok | Minor colour for "battle" songs with the same instrument tuning |
| do mi so do' | C4 E4 G4 C5 | 4, 3, 5 | none (all consonant) | all chord tones | E = maj7 | C = sus4, E = 6 | ok | Bugle-call set: most separable pitches, any pair consonant, but only one chord; needs rhythm for variety |
| la do mi la' (minor triad + octave) | A3 C4 E4 A4 | 3, 4, 5 | none | ok | ok | weak | chord tones | Same, minor |
| do so do' + re or la | C4 G4 C5 + D5 / A4 | 7, 5, 2 | M2 | ok | ok | ok | ok | Open-fifth set: no third, so the backing track decides major or minor; widest spacing |
| do re mi fa (tetrachord) | C4 D4 E4 F4 | 2, 2, 1 | semitone E-F | F clashes with E of chord | E clashes | F is b7, C clash | F clashes with E | Avoid: semitone, hardest discrimination, avoid-notes over I |
| OoT set minus one: re fa la ti | D4 F4 A4 B4 | 3, 4, 2 | tritone F-B | n/a (modal) | — | — | — | Colourful but contains a tritone |

- Recommended default (inference): do mi so la (C E G A, or transposed) or do re mi so. Both are pentatonic subsets, semitone- and tritone-free. do mi so la has larger spacing (4, 3, 2 semitones) and works equally as a major (C6) or relative-minor (Am7) set, so one tuning serves "happy" and "battle" backings. Its one weakness is the 2-semitone so-la gap.
- If discriminability is the priority (youngest players), do mi so do' (gaps 4, 3, 5) or a wider voicing such as do so do' mi' (C4 G4 C5 E5: gaps 7, 5, 4 semitones) keeps every neighbour at least a major third apart and every combination consonant.
- With the IMU as an octave or "shift" modifier, 4 buttons x 2 tilt states gives 8 pitches; mapping the tilted state to the same solfège an octave up preserves the button-to-scale-degree mapping (transfer), while mapping it to the missing pentatonic notes (re, la) gives a full pentatonic scale.
- V chord caveat: do (C) over a G major chord is the only regular clash in pentatonic sets (4th against the 3rd). Backing tracks can avoid it by voicing V as Gsus4, G5 (no third) or by using I, IV, vi and bVII instead of V.
- Count of distinct pitch orderings available with 4 buttons (ignoring rhythm): 4^3 = 64 three-note openings, 4^4 = 256 four-note, 4^6 = 4096 six-note; with no immediate repeats 4x3x3 = 36 three-note cells. Pitch alone is plenty for 12 to 30 songs; rhythm multiplies it.

## 3. Reharmonisation and "always consonant" backing tracks

### Takeaway
Fixed melody notes change meaning when the bass changes (same note becomes root, third, fifth, sixth or ninth), which is how OoT recolours five notes; the safest accompaniment for free 4-note input is a drone or non-functional harmony under a pentatonic set, the Orff model, and commercial games add timing quantisation and per-moment note substitution.

### Cited Findings
- Orff model: pentatonic melody over a bordun (open fifth 1-5, third omitted), giving non-functional harmony in which improvised notes do not clash. — [Orff Schulwerk (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orff_Schulwerk); [AOSA curriculum](https://member.aosa.org/storage/files/shares/CMS%20Pages/TE%20Content/Curriculum%20Docs/Recorder_Level%20II_v4.5_Nov%202019.pdf)
- Modal recolouring in OoT: same notes centred on D read as Dorian, centred on F as Lydian; the accompaniment (and an out-of-set F sharp in Serenade of Water) changes the mood. — [ThinkSpace Education](https://thinkspace.ac.uk/blog/a-lesson-in-modes-from-the-legend-of-zelda/)
- Wii Music: the team decided early "to rule out wrong sounds no matter how someone plays" (Kazumi Totaka); Miyamoto: play is about "enjoying limitless possibilities, all of which are correct" rather than performing accurately in time with a score. — [Iwata Asks: Wii Music, vol. 2](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/wii_music/1/2); [Nintendo UK, "No Score, No Mistakes"](https://www.nintendo.co.uk/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-Wii-Music/Volume-2-Wii-Music-A-First-Time-For-Everyone/3-No-Score-No-Mistakes/3-No-Score-No-Mistakes-238445.html) (from search extracts; direct fetch failed on a certificate error)
- Mechanism of Wii Music: each song has an invisible note chart; when the player gestures, "an appropriate note sounds" (the player controls timing, the game picks pitch), in contrast to Guitar Hero where the player only keeps up with fixed notes. — [Inverse, "Remembering Wii Music"](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/nintendo-wii-music-anniversary-15-years)
- Rez: players are in effect remixing the soundtrack (music and sound effects) by playing; the avatar pulses to the beat. Lumines Electronic Symphony: all sound events are synced to an overall music track. Mizuguchi says Lumines uses music as a reward rather than demanding rhythm timing; early Lumines interactive sound interfered with compositions, changed from Lumines Live! on. — [Designing Sound, "The feels of interactive audio"](https://designingsound.org/2016/11/22/the-feels-of-interactive-audio/); [Designing Sound, interview with Yuki Ichiki](https://designingsound.org/2013/03/interview-with-yuki-ichiki/); [Game Developer, Lumines Supernova interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/interview-nakamura-yokota-on-the-origins-of-i-lumines-supernova-i-)

### Inferences
- Three levels of safety for the backing, from simplest to richest:
  1. Drone/bordun: bass and pad hold do + so (C + G), no third. Every pentatonic button note is consonant at any time. Mood changes by rhythm, tempo, instrumentation only.
  2. Pedal with moving upper chords, or bass moving among do, la, fa (I, vi, IV; add bVII) under the same button notes: the fixed melody C E G A is heard as 1-3-5-6 over C, as b3-5-b7-1 over A (minor, "battle"), as 5-7-9-3 over F (bright/lydian-ish). Same motif, three moods. Avoid plain V with its third, or voice it sus4.
  3. Game-side substitution (Wii Music style): each button means "scale degree slot n of the current chord", and the engine swaps the sounded pitch per bar. This breaks the fixed button-to-pitch memory that the game is trying to teach, so it is not recommended for a memory game.
- For a reproduce-the-sequence game the pitch map should stay fixed and audible, and the backing during the child's turn should be thinned to drums + drone so the child's own notes are clearly heard; the full harmony can return when the game replays the completed song (the OoT pattern: player inputs 6 notes, then the orchestra answers).
- Timing quantisation (snapping the sounded note to the nearest subdivision, as in Rez/Lumines-type designs) improves how the result sounds but hides the child's real timing; a compromise is to sound the note immediately and judge with a tolerance window.
- Reharmonisation recipe for "battle" versions: keep the motif, move the bass to la (relative minor), raise tempo, use short percussive timbre, add a low ostinato on la-mi.

## 4. What makes a short motif memorable and distinguishable

### Takeaway
For short novel melodies, listeners, including infants and young children, encode contour (pattern of ups and downs) first and exact intervals later, so motifs should differ in contour and rhythm rather than in fine interval detail; repetition of a short cell, a common overall contour with one distinctive feature, and a livelier tempo all help.

### Cited Findings
- Dowling (1978): melodies are stored as a scale schema plus melodic contour; contour functions independently of exact interval sequence in memory and dominates immediate recognition of short novel melodies. — [Dowling 1978, "Scale and contour" (UT Dallas copy)](https://labs.utdallas.edu/mpac/files/2021/03/1978-2.pdf)
- Contour dominates mainly at short delays; with familiarity or longer delays interval/scale-step information matters more, and trained listeners encode scale steps (Dowling 1986). — [McDermott, Lehr & Oxenham 2008](https://mcdermottlab.mit.edu/papers/McDermott_Lehr_Oxenham_2008_relative_pitch.pdf); [review, PMC2841133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2841133)
- Infants and untrained adults readily detect contour changes but have more difficulty with interval changes in unfamiliar melodies; infants in Trehub, Bull & Thorpe (1984) did not distinguish exact transpositions of six-note melodies from same-contour lures. — [Trainor et al. 2002](https://trainorlab.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/trainor_et_al_2002.pdf); [PMC2841133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2841133)
- Young children correctly reproduce the contour of simple melodies before they reproduce the intervals correctly (Dowling 1982, as cited). — [PMC2841133](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2841133)
- Earworms (Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart & Müllensiefen 2017, 100 earworm tunes versus 100 matched controls, 83 melodic features): earworm tunes had faster tempi, more common global melodic contours, and less common average gradients between melodic turning points; press material glosses the last as unusual leaps or more repeated notes than usual. — [Goldsmiths repository](https://research.gold.ac.uk/19405); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/555535)
- Tone sequences can be recalled better than verbal sequences in forward span, apparently because of contour; the advantage disappears in backward span, where contour is disrupted (Schulze & Tillmann 2013, as discussed). — [Psychological Research 2021](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00426-021-01519-0.pdf)
- Melody identity is independent of key, tempo and timbre; memory for key and tempo fades faster than for timbre. — [Schellenberg & Habashi 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergHabachi2015.pdf)
- OoT practice: a 3-note cell repeated, then a longer answering phrase with opposite direction. — [Zelda Universe](https://zeldauniverse.net/features/the-brilliance-of-koji-kondos-sound-design-in-ocarina-of-time/)

### Inferences
- Design rules for a song library (mine, derived from the above):
  1. Give every song a unique (first note, contour of first 3 notes) pair. With 4 pitches there are 4 possible first notes and contours such as up-up, up-down, down-up, down-down, same-up, up-same, etc.
  2. Give every song a unique rhythm cell; two songs may share pitches if rhythm differs and vice versa, never both.
  3. Length ladder: 2 to 3 notes (tutorial), 3-note cell x2 (OoT child songs), 5 to 8 notes with internal repetition (later). Chunk longer songs as cell + cell + answer.
  4. End "question" phrases on so or la (open), "answer" phrases on do (closed); call and response between game and child.
  5. Keep one "surprise" per motif (a leap, a repeated note, a syncopation) on an otherwise common arch or descending contour.
- Example motifs for the set do mi so la = C4 E4 G4 A4 (buttons 1 to 4 low to high). Durations: q = quarter, e = eighth, h = half, dq = dotted quarter; tempo about 100 to 120 bpm. All are my own illustrations.
  - "Hello" (so-mi call): G q, E q, G e, G e, E q
  - "Rise" (up-up arpeggio, x2): C e, E e, G q | C e, E e, G q
  - "Fall" (down-down, long-short-short): A q, G e, E e, C h
  - "Hop" (leap up, step down, x2): C q, A q, G h | C q, A q, G h
  - "Gallop" (same pitch rhythm identity): E e, E e, G q, E e, E e, C q
  - "Question / Answer": G q, A q, G q, E q (open) / G q, E q, C h (closed)
  - "Battle" (over A minor bass, syncopated): A e, A e, C q, E dq, G e, A h (with the octave modifier for the last A, or keep A4)
  - "Lullaby" (3/4): E h, G q, A h, G q | E h, G q, C dotted-h
- Same cell, three identities by rhythm only (C E G): even q q q; long-short-short h e e; short-short-long e e h. This is the cheapest way to multiply the song count and it trains timing, which is the game's skill.

## 5. Pitch spacing for children and pitch-to-space mapping, including blind listeners

### Takeaway
Children can hear small pitch differences but labelling direction ("higher/lower", "up/down") is learned, unreliable in many 5- to 6-year-olds without training, and culture- and language-dependent; the vertical pitch-space association does exist in early-blind adults, so it does not depend on vision, but no data on blind children were found. Use wide spacing (thirds or more) and teach the mapping explicitly through play rather than assuming it.

### Cited Findings
- Stalinski, Schellenberg & Trehub (2008, JASA 124:1759-63): three-tone task, judge whether the middle tone is higher or lower; pitch changes from 4 down to 0.1 semitones; Experiment 1 tested twenty-six 5-year-olds without musical training; performance improved from age 5 to 8, when it became adult-like. — [paper PDF](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/StalinskiJASA2008.pdf); summary in [PMC6195045](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6195045)
- 5- and 6-year-olds can discriminate pitch-direction changes but do not typically apply the labels "higher", "lower", "up", "down" to pitch unless they receive targeted training. — [Saindon et al. 2016, J. Child Language](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SaindonEtAl2016.pdf)
- Frequency discrimination: large child-adult differences persist through school age; 6- to 7-year-olds have higher thresholds than 8- to 9-year-olds and adults; development may be non-monotonic and limited by immature pitch memory, with large individual differences (some 4-year-olds perform like adults). — [PMC3911822](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3911822); [PMC3339501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3339501)
- 5- to 9-year-olds rely less on relative-pitch cues than older children and adults. — [Stalinski & Schellenberg 2010](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/StalinskiSchellenberg2010.pdf)
- Height-pitch associations: Dutch 4-month-olds look longer at congruent rising visuals with rising tones (Dolscheid et al. 2014, as cited), yet nonlinguistic space-pitch associations were not consistent in 5-year-olds, who also understood height-pitch metaphors in reversed form (high pitch = "low"). — [PMC6152836](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6152836); [Dolscheid, Hunnius & Majid 2015, CogSci](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1v08v2x9)
- Height-pitch associations were not reliable in Dutch-speaking children until about age 11; Turkish-speaking children showed a reversed mapping; thickness-pitch associations (thin = high, thick = low) appeared earlier and were more robust across cultures. — [Oxford Experimental Psychology record, 2023](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publication/1299933); [ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:de15b129-68c2-4083-85ee-2a0bbbc22c69)
- Early-blind adults: in a SMARC-type task (timbre judgement with vertically arranged response keys), blind and sighted groups showed a comparable pitch-height to vertical-space association, "suggesting that the lack of prior visual experience does not prevent the development of an association between pitch height and vertical space". — [Cattaneo et al. 2018, Attention, Perception & Psychophysics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-018-1495-x) (from search extract; full text behind login redirect)
- Conflicting evidence: Deroy et al. (2016) found blind individuals showed no preferential association between pitch and the direction of tactile movement, whereas sighted individuals did; impaired vertical sound localisation in blind people may also affect pitch-vertical mapping. — [Deroy et al. 2016, JEP:HPP](https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/6569/1/OD-ET-AL-JEP-16.pdf); discussed in [Cattaneo et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13414-018-1495-x)

### Inferences
- Spacing: discrimination thresholds are far below a semitone even in children, so the limit is not hearing but labelling, memory and confusability under time pressure. Neighbouring buttons a major second apart (re-mi, so-la) are the likely confusion pairs; minor thirds and larger are safer. For ages 5 to 7 prefer sets with all gaps of 3 semitones or more (do mi so do', or do so do' mi').
- Mapping: do not rely on "up = higher" being intuitive for 5- to 7-year-olds or on left-to-right = low-to-high (a keyboard convention). Choose one consistent mapping and teach it by exploration (free play where each button always gives the same note), not by verbal instruction. The earlier, more robust "thick/big = low, thin/small = high" association suggests reinforcing pitch with a second cue: lower notes with a rounder, bigger timbre and stronger/lower-frequency haptic pulse, higher notes thinner and lighter.
- Redundant cues per button (pitch + slight stereo pan position matching the button + distinct vibration pattern) make the task robust for children whose pitch-direction labelling is immature. Panning is a spatial cue available to blind listeners that does not depend on the pitch-height metaphor.
- If the IMU is used for octave, tilting "up" for the higher octave matches the adult vertical SMARC effect that is preserved in early-blind adults, but it should be verified by playtest with the target children.

### Gaps
- No study of SMARC or pitch-space mapping in congenitally blind children was found (explicit null result of the search).
- Rusconi et al. (2006), the original SMARC paper, was not retrieved. From recall (UNVERIFIED): the vertical effect appeared in both musicians and non-musicians, while the horizontal left = low / right = high effect was reliable mainly in musicians. Must be confirmed.
- The exact percent-correct by interval size for 5-year-olds in Stalinski et al. (2008) could not be extracted (PDF not machine-readable here), so no numeric "minimum reliable interval for 5-year-olds" is given.

## 6. Timbre, attack time and register

### Takeaway
Rhythmic precision needs sounds with fast attack and short duration: their perceived beat location (P-centre) is close to physical onset and narrow, whereas slow-attack, long sounds (bowed strings, pads, soft flute) are heard tens of milliseconds late and with a wide "beat bin".

### Cited Findings
- Perceptual attack time (PAT) is the moment a sound's rhythmic emphasis is heard; it differs from physical and perceptual onset; for a slowly bowed violin note it may be as much as 50 to 100 ms after physical onset. — [Perceptual attack time (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptual_attack_time)
- Gordon (1987, JASA 82:88-105) measured PAT for 16 resynthesised orchestral instrument tones; PAT depends on rise time and listening level (slope of the rise is the key factor), with duration and spectrum weaker cues. — [Perceptual attack time (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptual_attack_time)
- Shorter rise times give earlier P-centres, longer rise times later ones (Vos & Rasch 1981; Vos et al. 1995). — [Frontiers in Psychology 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00080/full)
- Danielsen et al. (2019, JEP:HPP 45(3):402-418): attack and duration are the primary cues for P-centre location and variability; fast-attack, short sounds give narrow peaks close to onset; slow-attack, long sounds give wider, flatter distributions; P-centres are "beat bins", not points, and depend on the stimulus and the synchronisation task. — [JYX repository record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/63979?show=full)
- Melody memory is largely independent of timbre, but timbre is remembered longer than key or tempo. — [Schellenberg & Habashi 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergHabachi2015.pdf)
- Children aged 9 to 11 remember sung (vocal) melodies better than instrumental ones. — [Weiss, Schellenberg, Trehub & Dawber 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/WeissEtAl2015.pdf)

### Inferences
- Good instrument candidates (fast attack, clear pitch, natural decay so notes do not smear): marimba/xylophone, kalimba, glockenspiel or celesta (higher register), plucked strings (harp, pizzicato, guitar, koto), piano, steel pan. Use with care: flute/ocarina, bowed strings, choir "ah", pads; if included for flavour, give them a synthesised hard attack (a short percussive transient layered on the onset) and shift the sample earlier so the P-centre, not the file start, lands on the button press.
- Scoring implication: if instruments differ in attack, the timing-judgement window should be referenced to each instrument's P-centre (a per-instrument offset of roughly 0 to 50+ ms), or the child will be judged "late" on slow instruments when imitating what they heard.
- Bells with inharmonic partials (tubular bells, some glockenspiel samples) have a clear attack but ambiguous pitch; fine for rhythm, weaker for pitch identity.
- Register: place button notes roughly C4 to C6. Small speakers reproduce little below about 200 to 300 Hz (C4 is 262 Hz), and pitch direction is easier to follow in the mid-high range; keep bass/drone parts an octave or more below the playable notes, with enough upper harmonics to survive small speakers, so that player notes are never masked.
- A sung solfège or "la" voice as one selectable instrument is supported by the vocal-melody memory advantage, but sung notes have slower, vowel-dependent P-centres; use short consonant-initial syllables (do, mi, so, la all begin with a consonant; the P-centre sits near the vowel onset).

### Gaps
- Numeric P-centre offsets per instrument (ms) from Danielsen et al. (2019) and Gordon (1987) could not be extracted from the PDFs here.
- No source was retrieved for small-speaker/headphone frequency response versus melody audibility; the C4 to C6 recommendation is engineering common sense, not a cited finding.
- No source was found on timbre preferences or discrimination in blind children.

## 7. Alternatives to pitch-per-button (percussion, syllables, animal sounds, sung solfège)

### Takeaway
Contour is what makes tone sequences easy to remember in order, and sung melodies are remembered better than instrumental ones by children, so pitched (ideally optionally sung) buttons are the best default; unpitched percussion or animal sounds are maximally discriminable per button and good for a first tutorial stage or an accessibility mode, but lose contour and melodic transfer.

### Cited Findings
- Forward recall can be better for tone sequences than verbal sequences, attributed to contour; verbal sequences win in backward recall. Verbal and tonal short-term memory appear partly separate (double dissociations reported). — [Psychological Research 2021, discussing Schulze & Tillmann 2013](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00426-021-01519-0.pdf)
- Vocal melodies are remembered better than instrumental melodies by 9- to 11-year-olds. — [Weiss et al. 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/WeissEtAl2015.pdf)
- Timbre information is retained in melody memory longer than key or tempo. — [Schellenberg & Habashi 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergHabachi2015.pdf)
- Kodály practice pairs each pitch with a solfège syllable (and hand sign), i.e. a verbal label per scale degree, as the core memory aid. — [Kodály method](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/Kodály_Method)

### Inferences
| Mapping | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| 4 pitches, one timbre | Contour chunking; real melodies; transfers across instruments; OoT model | Neighbouring pitches confusable for the youngest; needs pitch-to-button learning |
| 4 percussion timbres (kick, snare, hat, clap) | Categorical, near-zero confusion; sharp attacks, best timing; no harmony problem | No contour; sequences are harder to chunk and to sing back; less "song" identity |
| 4 animal or object sounds | Fun, nameable (child can rehearse verbally: "dog, cat, cat, cow") | Long, slow-attack samples blur rhythm; little musical reward |
| Sung solfège voice (do, mi, so, la) | Double coding (pitch + syllable); vocal advantage; child can sing along; builds real musical literacy | Language/recording cost; slower P-centre; syllables may distract some |
| Pitch + per-button timbre/pan/haptic cue | Redundant coding, robust for weak pitch labelling | Slightly busier sound |

- Suggested progression: begin with highly distinct sounds or wide pitch gaps, then move to the pentatonic pitch set; offer the sung-solfège instrument as a selectable "voice". A fixed pitch map across all instruments lets a song learned on one instrument be replayed on another.

### Gaps
- No direct experimental comparison of pitch versus percussion versus verbal mappings in a Simon-style reproduction game for children was found.
- Evidence on verbal rehearsal benefits for blind children specifically was not searched.

## 8. Tuning and key choices

### Takeaway
No retrieved evidence suggests temperament matters for this use; the practical decisions are to keep one fixed key and button-to-scale-degree map across all instruments so songs transfer, and to pick the key by register and by what the backing tracks need.

### Cited Findings
- Melody identity survives changes of key, tempo and timbre (listeners recognise the tune), so transposition does not destroy a learned song; absolute key memory fades fastest. — [Schellenberg & Habashi 2015](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/SchellenbergHabachi2015.pdf)
- 5- to 9-year-olds rely less on relative pitch than older listeners, i.e. they attend more to absolute pitch level. — [Stalinski & Schellenberg 2010](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/StalinskiSchellenberg2010.pdf)
- Orff/Kodály practice uses a fixed pentatonic bar set over a tonic drone, i.e. one key at a time. — [Orff Schulwerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orff_Schulwerk)

### Inferences
- Because young children lean on absolute pitch more than adults, keeping the same absolute pitches on the same buttons across instruments (octave shifts allowed) is safer than transposing per instrument.
- Key choice: C or D pentatonic puts do mi so la at C4-A4 or D4-B4, inside children's singing range (roughly C4 to A4/C5) so they can sing along; G or F with so below do (so la do re mi) suits plagal folk tunes. One global key simplifies backing tracks, sample sets and haptic cue design.
- Equal temperament versus just intonation: in a pentatonic set the differences are at most about 14 to 16 cents (major third and major sixth), far smaller than the 200 to 700 cent gaps between buttons, so it does not affect discrimination. Use 12-TET so sampled instruments and backing tracks stay in tune with each other; just intonation is only worth considering for a pure drone-plus-voice mode where sustained thirds beat audibly.
- If "battle" songs need a darker colour, change the bass (to la) rather than the key or the button notes.

### Gaps
- No empirical source was retrieved on temperament perception in children or on optimal keys for children's game audio; this section is reasoning from first principles plus the cited memory findings.
- Children's comfortable singing range figures are from general pedagogy recall and were not sourced in this session.
